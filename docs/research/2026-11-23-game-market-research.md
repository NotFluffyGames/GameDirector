# Research Before Ideation Should Hand Over Comps, Anchors and a Player, Not a Market Report

In indie practice, pre-production market research produces a short positioning package. It holds a few comparable titles ("comps") with dated, sourced estimates. It names the features players of the genre expect, which Chris Zukowski calls "anchors", and the departure that makes this game worth noticing, its "hook". It describes the player by motivations rather than demographics, records the platform, price and session norms the comps set, and lists the risks those numbers imply. The parts that change the design are the genre frame and its anchors, the comps' recurring complaints, and the platform and session norms. Sales estimates and saturation counts change scope, budget and the go/no-go call, and they matter only for a game meant to sell. The methods are practitioner methods published as blog posts and talks, and their numbers are soft. The review-count multiplier named after Mike Boxleiter has fallen from a 30–150× range in 2018 to roughly 20–60× for games launched since 2020, because Steam began prompting players for reviews in late 2019. Wishlist-to-sales ratios vary up to a hundredfold between games. SteamSpy has reported model-based estimates since 2018. Motivation models split into two kinds. "Universals" (Self-Determination Theory and its PENS questionnaire) describe what any satisfying game must give. "Differentiators" (Quantic Foundry's twelve motivations) describe who a particular game is for. Bartle's types describe MUD players, and the online "Bartle Test" was written by other people. An agent can legitimately get most comps data from Steam's first-party endpoints and a few documented third-party APIs. From this session, however, every Steam, SteamSpy, itch.io and app-store host was blocked. The phase therefore needs those hosts allowed; otherwise it falls back to search summaries and the person's own references. Five things stay with the person: commercial intent, risk appetite, which comps count as "us", which anchors to break, and every reference pick. Validation tests that need a public page (wishlists, Steam Playtest, Next Fest, ad tests) belong after the vertical slice, not before ideation. Claude-Code-Game-Studios has no research step. It folds comps, player types, references and market risks into its concept template, and it forbids market numbers quoted from memory. For GameDirector the evidence points to a short, autonomous research step between setup and ideation. A commercial-intent flag asked at setup would size it, and its brief would feed ideation's directions and the roles' north stars.

## A research phase produces a positioning package, and only part of it changes the design

The talks and guides that describe this work agree on its shape. Victoria Tran (Kitfox Games) and Dana Trebella (Spoke and Wheel) taught "day 0 marketing" at GDC Summer 2020 in three steps: market research, a competitor analysis, and a positioning document, aimed at judging "if your game idea is one worth pursuing" ([GDC Vault](https://gdcvault.com/play/1026850/Pre-Marketing-Marketing-A-Guide)). A Tran slide deck lists pre-production goals as competitor analysis, SWOT and customer profiles, across platforms, genre saturation, audience size and viability ([Google Slides deck](https://docs.google.com/presentation/d/1TIUQiJdLPJZT8UnuWKil_kyMeUuIAwIT/htmlpresent), *search summary*; not confirmed to be the same talk). In 2026 Trebella and Derek Lieu showed how SWOT, market research and audience research turn into trailers and assets ([GDC Vault](https://gdcvault.com/play/1035707/Back-to-Basics-Translating-Your)). Rogers' one-sheet in *Level Up!* carries target players, a rating, unique selling points and "competitive products" ([O'Reilly](https://www.oreilly.com/library/view/level-up-the/9780470688670/); field list from a [course summary](https://ysjiosgames.netlify.app/docs/gamedev-2), *search summary*). VG Insights' co-founder Karl Kontus lists the questions such research should settle before development. They cover which genres are oversupplied or dominated by hobby projects, who the players are and why they play, "what are other similar games doing wrong?", price elasticity, platforms, localisation and release timing ([Game Developer, 2021](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/77-questions-that-every-indie-developer-should-ask)).

Comps are gathered by proximity. One guide recommends a day on Steam, then the "Related games" of each close match, sorted into rings from "most like yours" to "some things in common". For each comp it records sales, reviews, tags, mechanics and post-mortems, and it warns against aiming for broad appeal ([Michael Wagner, Game Developer, 2021](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/marketing-for-indie-devs-market-research)). Pitch guides commonly ask for three to five comps from the last two or three years, mixing a hit, a middling seller and a weak one, and they warn against comparing a small team to giants ([generalistprogrammer](https://generalistprogrammer.com/tutorials/how-to-pitch-a-game-to-publishers), *secondary only*; no publisher's own submission guide could be read).

| Deliverable | What it contains | Design decision it changes | Evidence that it changes it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Genre frame | The 1–3 sub-genres or tags the game sits in; how crowded each is; share of games in it that earn above a threshold | Which game to make at all; scope and budget | Weinbaum: "the most important marketing decision for a game developer is the kind of game they choose to make", and "games sell within the scale of their genre's market size" ([Game Developer, 2019](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/genre-viability-on-steam-and-other-trends---an-analysis-using-review-count)) |
| Comps table | 5–10 titles: year, price, top tags, review count and score, estimated units as a range, playtime at review, praised and complained-about themes | Content volume, session length, price; a list of known pitfalls | Negative Steam reviews are mostly about design (below); comps set the shopper's expectations |
| Genre anchors and the genre default | The features players of this genre expect, with which comps show them; what most games of this kind do | Must-have features, and the deliberate departure | Shoppers decide by genre fit and read the UI in screenshots to identify it ([Zukowski, Game Developer, 2019](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/how-steam-users-see-your-game)) |
| Hook and positioning line | One phrase pairing the anchor genre with the hook ("a soulslike metroidvania"); a positioning statement | Which feature carries the game's identity; what polish must not remove | The hook-and-anchor exercise "collapses" a game's scope into its core ([David Logan, Akupara Games, Game Developer, 2020](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/indie-insights-hooks-and-anchors)) |
| Player profile | Primary and secondary motivations, who it is not for, the games they already play; the universal needs the loop must meet | Which loops and systems to deepen; what to leave out | Motivations differ systematically between audiences, and some genres combine unrelated motivations ([Nick Yee, GDC 2017](https://gdcvault.com/play/1024048/The-Anatomy-of-Gaming-Motivations)) |
| Platform, input, price and session expectations | What comps on the target platform charge, how long people play them before reviewing, and what input they assume | Controls, UI density, session structure, length | "Hot PC genres do not necessarily map that well at all to hot console game genres" ([Carless, Game Developer, 2021](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/which-genre-should-your-next-pc-game-be-in-2)) |
| Store-legibility requirements | The tags the game would carry; what its first four screenshots must show | What the vertical slice must make visible and capturable | The Steam hover tooltip cycles the first four screenshots and shows the top five tags ([Zukowski, 2019](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/how-steam-users-see-your-game)) |
| Market risks and validation plan | Saturation, budget-versus-comp revenue, discoverability risks; the signals that would confirm interest | Kill condition, go/no-go, when to show the game | Baseline organic wishlists predict launches better than total wishlists ([Carless, 2020](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/the-one-key-steam-stat-that-you-re-not-checking-2)) |

Two common outputs change nothing and are best left out. A total-market (TAM) figure "says nothing about what this game will sell", which is why CCGS's pitch template forbids it ([CCGS pitch-document.md](https://github.com/Donchitos/Claude-Code-Game-Studios/blob/main/.claude/docs/templates/pitch-document.md)). Demographic labels such as "core gamers" are the second: SteamSpy's creator Sergey Galyonkin argued that such audiences "don't technically exist" in any actionable way, and that Steam's buying is concentrated in roughly 1.3 million heavy buyers ([MCV](https://www.mcvuk.com/core-gamers-dont-technically-exist/), *secondary*; the original essay is on Medium, which was blocked).

## Indie and studio teams use review counts, tags, store behaviour and motivation models

### Review counts stand in for sales, with a multiplier that has drifted

Steam publishes review counts but not sales, so developers multiply reviews by a ratio. Jake Birkett (Grey Alien Games) first published the method in 2014, "before SteamSpy". He then found 30–100× with 70× in the middle across indie and AAA titles, and noted that "someone mentioned the Boxleiter number was 50x" ([Birkett, Game Developer, 2018](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/using-steam-reviews-to-estimate-sales)). Simon Carless's later survey says the ratio is named after Mike Boxleiter ([Carless, Game Developer, 2020](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/how-that-game-sold-on-steam-using-the-nb-number-2)). Boxleiter's own original statement and its figure could not be found, so the origin is **unverified**: the published lineage starts with Birkett's 2014 post ([greyaliengames.com](https://greyaliengames.com/blog/how-to-estimate-how-many-sales-a-steam-game-has-made/), blocked).

| Source | Sample | Stated ratio (units per review) | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Birkett 2014 ([via Birkett 2018](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/using-steam-reviews-to-estimate-sales)) | Indie and AAA | 30–100×, middle 70× | Original post blocked |
| Birkett 2018 ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/using-steam-reviews-to-estimate-sales)) | 50+ premium indie games | Mean 82×, median 77×, range 30–150×; launches after 2014 mean 71×, median 64×; 2017 launches median 65× (8 games) | Counts Steam sales against the store's "All Reviews", which excludes key activations; roguelikes, sims and strategy run higher, story-led adventure and puzzle lower |
| Carless 2020, "NB number" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/how-that-game-sold-on-steam-using-the-nb-number-2)) | 237 games, survey ([call for data](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/audio/help-needed-what-s-the-real-steam-reviews-sales-ratio-2)) | Mean 63×, median 58×, middle 80% 25–100×; pre-2017 launches median 74×; 2019 median 51×; 2020 median 38×, range 20–60× | Steam added a "would you like to review this?" prompt at the end of October 2019, after 2–3 hours of play |
| Kontus / VG Insights 2021 ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/how-to-estimate-steam-video-game-sales-in-2021-)) | 10,000+ games (leaked 2018 data) | 2020+ launches 20–55×, best guess 30×; pre-2014 launches 40–100× | Reviews, release year and free-to-play explain 78% of variance; ranges right for over 80% of games; higher prices lower the ratio; MMOs higher, sports and racing lower |

Three caveats travel with every estimate. First, choose the ratio by launch year, because a lifetime ratio mixes eras: one game's lifetime ratio was 30× while its 2020-only ratio was 14× ([Carless, 2020](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/how-that-game-sold-on-steam-using-the-nb-number-2)). Second, units are not revenue. After regional pricing, discounts, refunds, VAT and Steam's cut, net is "realistically 30%–50%" of units times list price (same source). Danny Weinbaum's working factor was 0.38, and he put his own estimates at "50% to 300% of the actual figure" ([Game Developer, 2019](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/genre-viability-on-steam-and-other-trends---an-analysis-using-review-count)). Third, report a range rather than a point, as Birkett advised: "1000 reviews could mean 30,000 to 150,000 sales" ([2018](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/using-steam-reviews-to-estimate-sales)).

### Saturation is read as the share of a genre that succeeds, not the count of games in it

Medians mislead in boom-or-bust genres. Weinbaum ranked Steam tags by the share of games that grossed over $200,000. He treated games with fewer than 10 reviews as "of no concern" to a career developer's competition, and distrusted tags with fewer than 30 games (too little data) or more than 2,000 (too vague, "indie" being the extreme) ([Game Developer, 2019](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/genre-viability-on-steam-and-other-trends---an-analysis-using-review-count)). Carless read the results this way. Some microgenres (wrestling, submarine) sell because "competition is EXTREMELY limited" but demand expertise. Platformer had 1,166 tagged games, of which 7% grossed over $200k, against FPS at 655 games and 21% ([Game Developer, 2020](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/analyzing-the-top-steam-tags-2)). A later tag table from the Games-Stats site put colony sim ($44k median net), roguelike deckbuilder ($38k), 4X ($35k) and city builder ($22k) at the top, against a median of all tag medians of about $2,600 (as cited by [Carless, 2021](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/which-genre-should-your-next-pc-game-be-in-2)). On volume, roughly 1,000 games launched on Steam each month in 2023 ([Schwab, GDC 2023 abstract](https://gdcvault.com/play/1029411/Validate-Hype-Release-A-Strategic)). Of 965 October 2021 releases, about 6% got more than 100 reviews in their first week ([Carless, Game Developer, 2021](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/marketing/game-devs-what-s-your-chance-of-success-in-today-s-market-)). Only about 10% of Steam's roughly 44,000 developers have grossed over $100k. Those that cleared $1m average 4–5 released games, and about half their games are strategy or simulation, against 36% for developers who grossed under $10k ([Kontus, Game Developer, 2022](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/what-can-we-learn-from-the-1-600-highest-earning-indie-developers-on-steam-)). Weinbaum's own caution holds for agents: "individual case studies are far more important for market research overall", and tags are "simplistic, inconsistent".

### Competitors' negative reviews show unmet needs, and mostly they are about design

The best evidence is an academic study of 10,954,956 Steam reviews of 6,224 games ([Lin, Bezemer, Zou & Hassan, *Empirical Software Engineering*, 2019](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s10664-018-9627-4); figures read from [PC Gamer's report](https://www.pcgamer.com/steam-study-finds-users-leave-negative-reviews-more-quickly-than-positive-ones/), *secondary*, since the paper was blocked). It estimates that 42% of reviews carry specific pros, cons or bug reports. In negative reviews, 57% mention game design and 17% mention bugs. Most negative reviews come within the first seven hours of play, while most reviews overall are written after about 13 hours. Reviews of indie games hold more design suggestions. Shoppers use reviews the same way. In Zukowski's observation study, participants read only negative reviews, looking for flaws in "the things that they actually care about", and discounted rants ([Game Developer, 2019](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/how-steam-users-see-your-game); seven paid participants, *single source*). For an agent, the method follows directly: pull each comp's negative reviews filtered by playtime, cluster the complaints, and record the ones the new design should avoid. No single originating source for "mine competitors' negative reviews" as a named indie method was found. Zukowski's competitive-analysis post ([howtomarketagame.com, 2019](https://howtomarketagame.com/2019/12/11/how-i-do-competitive-analysis-for-my-game/)) was blocked.

### Genre anchors and the hook: familiar first, then one departure

Zukowski calls anchors the qualities that make a game "familiar to audiences", while the hook "is what makes your game unique". His example is that a metroidvania needs a map. He found anchors from public Steam data and says a developer can knowingly break one ([howtomarketagame.com, 2020](https://howtomarketagame.com/2020/01/06/how-to-find-your-anchors/), *search summary*). A 2023 editorial of his reports that 88.2% of the hit games he studied "adhered more to predefined genre expectations" ([howtomarketagame.com](https://howtomarketagame.com/2023/02/22/editorial-maybe-mixing-genres-is-a-bad-idea/), *search summary, single source*). Studios use the pair as a design filter as well as a message. Akupara Games writes a short phrase per title that states the anchor ("GRIME is a soulslike metroidvania") and pairs it with one hook, which may be mechanical, aesthetic or narrative ([Logan, 2020](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/indie-insights-hooks-and-anchors)). Shoppers in Zukowski's study treated a resemblance to genre peers as a plus, for example "an FTL-like", and dropped games on "poison pill" tags ([2019](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/how-steam-users-see-your-game)). Design research outside games supports the balance: across industrial products, typicality and novelty jointly explained 56% of aesthetic preference while suppressing each other's effect ([Hekkert, Snelders & van Wieringen, *British Journal of Psychology*, 2003](https://research.vu.nl/en/publications/most-advanced-yet-acceptable-typicality-and-novelty-as-joint-pred/), *search summary*). A positioning statement in the form "For [target] who [need], [product] is a [category] that [benefit]; unlike [alternative], it [difference]" is usually attributed to Geoffrey Moore's *Crossing the Chasm* (1991). Its exact wording in the book could not be checked ([Umbrex](https://umbrex.com/resources/frameworks/marketing-frameworks/positioning-statement-framework-for-target-who-need-our-brand-is/), *secondary only*).

### Wishlists, store pages, Steam Playtest and Next Fest validate a game that already has a page

These are tests of a public game, not of an idea. For first-week sales against launch wishlists, Carless's 50-game survey found a median of 0.2 sales per wishlist and a mean of 0.36. Among games with more than 10,000 wishlists, the top half converted 12.3% in week one and the bottom half 4.5%. He notes that Valve officially cites 13% ([Game Developer, 2020](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/steam-the-new-wishlists-to-first-week-sales-expectations)). Wishlists bought by festivals and features convert worse ([Carless, 2020](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/we-need-to-take-steam-wishlist-quality-more-seriously-2)). A better pre-release signal is "baseline daily wishlists", organic additions outside events, which ranged from near zero to 200 a day. Carless reports Valve saying "on the record" that sales cannot be extrapolated from wishlists ([2020](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/the-one-key-steam-stat-that-you-re-not-checking-2)). In Zukowski's Next Fest surveys, the strongest predictor of wishlists gained during the festival was the wishlists held before it (February 2026, 182 responses) ([howtomarketagame.com](https://howtomarketagame.com/2025/03/26/benchmarks-how-many-wishlists-can-i-get-from-steam-next-fest/), *search summary*). Valve's Next Fest rules require a public store page and a demo playable when the festival starts. They place no requirement on a demo's length or newness, and since October 2024 a title may take part only once ([Steamworks](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/marketing/upcoming_events/nextfest), *search summary*). Steam Playtest is a free child app whose sign-ups appear on the main store page ([Steamworks](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/features/playtest), *search summary*). Earlier tests exist that do not need a build. Coldwild Games posted pixel art on Twitter and designed *Merchant of the Skies* "around the style that audiences liked before they even saw the actual gameplay" ([Slav, GDC 2020](https://gdcvault.com/play/1026601/Aesthetic-Driven-Development-Choosing-Your)). Jason Della Rocca argues for community validation "earlier than early access" ([GDC 2022](https://gdcvault.com/play/1027884/Community-Validated-Deployment-Why-You)). All of these need an account, a public presence and the developer's decision to show the game.

### Player motivation models answer two different questions

Nick Yee frames motivation in two ways. A "universals" approach asks what all players need, and a "differentiators" approach asks how players differ ([GDC 2021](https://gdcvault.com/play/1027402/Universals-vs-Differentiators-A-Tale)). The models below sort cleanly into those two kinds.

| Model | Original source | What it answers | Use in research | Caveats |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Quantic Foundry Gamer Motivation Model | Survey of over 140,000 gamers at its first public presentation ([Yee, GDC 2016](https://gdcvault.com/play/1023242/The-Gamer-Motivation-Profile-Model)); now "2M+" respondents ([QF reference sheet v3, 2026](https://quanticfoundry.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/Gamer-Motivation-Model-Reference-v3.pdf), *search summary*) | Who the game is for: 12 motivations in 6 pairs, Action (Destruction, Excitement), Social (Competition, Community), Mastery (Challenge, Strategy), Achievement (Completion, Power), Immersion (Fantasy, Story), Creativity (Design, Discovery) (*search summary*) | Name the 2–3 motivations the comps and the core loop serve, and those deliberately not served | Some motivations are "much more volatile than others", which bears on audience coverage and risk, and some genres are "accidental historical combinations of unrelated gaming motivations" ([GDC 2017](https://gdcvault.com/play/1024048/The-Anatomy-of-Gaming-Motivations)); cluster-based segments with typical titles ([GDC 2020](https://gdcvault.com/play/1026849/Player-Segments-Based-on-Gaming)); one 2021 study confirmed the factor structure only in part (*search summary*) |

Bartle: the Bartle Test is "the brainchild of Erwin S. Andreasen and Brandon A. Downey", not his. ### Personas work when built from behaviour and play, not demographics

Alan Cooper began using a prototype persona in 1983 and popularised the technique in *The Inmates Are Running the Asylum* (1999) ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persona_(user_experience)), *search summary*). Game research adapted it as "play-personas", archetypes of how people play, derived from behaviour and telemetry ([Canossa & Drachen, DiGRA 2009](https://dl.digra.org/index.php/dl/article/view/399), *search summary*). Practitioners describe indie buyers by what they already play rather than by age. Zukowski's participants "knew exactly what type of games they like and don't like" and would not cross genres for any copy or trailer, and he recruited heavy buyers because, citing Galyonkin, indie games sell to a thin slice of players with large libraries ([2019](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/how-steam-users-see-your-game)). A persona for an agent is therefore best written as "plays X, Y and Z; wants motivations A and B; leaves at C".

### Mobile, console and web have their own tests

Mobile stores offer first-party A/B tests of store pages. Apple's product page optimization tests up to three alternate pages against the original on a random share of traffic for up to 90 days, and estimates the impressions needed for 90% confidence ([Apple](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/product-page-optimization/)). Apple pre-orders open 2–180 days before a new app's release ([Apple](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/pre-orders/)). Google Play's store listing experiments test up to two variants and can measure pre-registration clicks ([Google Play Console](https://play.google.com/console/about/store-listing-experiments/), *search summary*). Mobile teams also run "marketability" or CPI tests: ads for a prototype measure cost per install before production. One publisher's guidance puts good Android marketability below $0.25 CPI ([Supersonic](https://supersonic.com/learn/blog/building-and-improving-creatives-for-marketability-testing-a-hyper-casual-developers-guide/), *search summary, vendor, single source*). Soft launches in test markets then measure retention and revenue ([Keith Katz, GDC 2015](https://gdcvault.com/play/1022205/Playing-Your-Own-Game-How)). For consoles, no public sales or review data comparable to Steam's was found, and Carless warns that PC genre data does not transfer ([2021](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/which-genre-should-your-next-pc-game-be-in-2)). On the web, itch.io turns any browse page into an RSS feed by appending `.xml`, which makes tag and price lists machine-readable ([itch.io](https://itch.io/updates/rss-feeds-for-browsing-games), *search summary*). Web portals' own developer data (Poki, CrazyGames) was **not researched**.

## References are gathered per aspect, each with what to take and what to leave

Comps and references do different jobs. Comps describe the market the game enters. References describe the qualities the team wants. CCGS's concept template records each reference as "What We Take From It | What We Do Differently | Why It Matters", and asks for non-game inspirations ([CCGS game-concept.md](https://github.com/Donchitos/Claude-Code-Game-Studios/blob/main/.claude/docs/templates/game-concept.md)). GameDirector's vision template already asks for "two or three games, and exactly what this one takes from each, and what it does differently" (`skills/game-ideation/vision-template.md`). The companion roles research covers the craft side in depth ([2026-11-23-game-development-roles.md](2026-11-23-game-development-roles.md)): art boards sorted by purpose (shape, palette, lighting, UI), audio references plus anti-references, and one to three timestamped clips per core verb for feel. The research phase adds two things to that practice. Josie Devora (Fighting Chance) builds discovery boards and a competitive analysis together, so the art style is chosen to "position your game" against its genre peers ([GDC 2023](https://gdcvault.com/play/1028955/Art-Direction-Summit-Finding-Your)). For music, a reference track becomes usable only once the team puts words to what works in it, such as instruments, emotion, tempo and pacing ([Kent Carter, Game Developer, 2020](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/audio/video-game-music-a-developer-s-guide-to-finding-the-perfect-track)). The known risk is attachment: a temp track can steer the composer into imitation ([Wikipedia: Temp track](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temp_track), *search summary*).

| Aspect | Typical reference set | Record for each | Avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Play (loop, systems) | 2–4 games, usually overlapping the comps | The mechanic or structure borrowed; the anchor it represents; the comps' complaints about it | Copying a comp's loop whole; it becomes the genre default with no hook |
| Look | Games plus non-game art, sorted by purpose | Shape, palette, value or UI rule taken; what not to take ("their noise level") | A board of moods with no rule an asset could fail |
| Sound | Tracks and game soundscapes; anti-references | Instruments, emotion, tempo, what the player must hear | Temp-track attachment |
| Feel | Clips per core verb | Timestamp, the adjective it shows, timing to match | References the engine or input device cannot match |
| Tone and world | Games, films, books | The tone words, and the words to avoid | References whose names or art would ship: CCGS warns that with a selling goal "the reference's name, art and logos cannot ship" ([one-shot-3-handover.md](https://github.com/Donchitos/Claude-Code-Game-Studios/blob/main/.claude/skills/prototype/references/one-shot-3-handover.md)) |

## Claude-Code-Game-Studios folds market lines into its concept, with no research step

CCGS 1.3.0 has no market-research skill or phase. Its market and audience content sits inside `/brainstorm`'s full-concept flow and its templates:

- **Market lines.** A "Market lines" rule says any claim about sales, trends or audience size "is stale or invented when it comes from memory": it must cite a source or say "not researched" ([full-concept.md](https://github.com/Donchitos/Claude-Code-Game-Studios/blob/main/.claude/skills/brainstorm/references/full-concept.md)). `WebSearch` is in the skill's tools ([SKILL.md](https://github.com/Donchitos/Claude-Code-Game-Studios/blob/main/.claude/skills/brainstorm/SKILL.md)), but no step tells it to gather comps.
- **Concepts and player types.** Each generated concept has a hook in "like X, and also Y" form. Phase 5, "Player types", names the primary player from "Bartle's types and the Quantic Foundry model", plus secondary appeal, who it is not for, and games that serve the same player.
- **Concept template.** The template ([game-concept.md](https://github.com/Donchitos/Claude-Code-Game-Studios/blob/main/.claude/docs/templates/game-concept.md)) has Comparable Titles (2–3, "any sales figure with its source, or 'not researched'"), a Target Player Profile ("'Gamers' is not a target audience": age, experience, time, platform, current games, unmet need, dealbreakers), an Inspiration and References table, and Market Risks.
- **Pitch template.** The pitch template asks for "Why now", comparable sales as a sourced range, and no TAM ([pitch-document.md](https://github.com/Donchitos/Claude-Code-Game-Studios/blob/main/.claude/docs/templates/pitch-document.md)).
- **Creative director.** The `creative-director` agent is told to keep a "positioning map that plots the game against comparable titles on 2-3 key axes" and a reference library ([creative-director.md](https://github.com/Donchitos/Claude-Code-Game-Studios/blob/main/.claude/agents/creative-director.md)).
- **Genre sheets.** Its "chipsets" come closest to an anchors list: one sheet per genre of first-minute defaults, each with a "Why" and a "Break it when". The sheets are labelled "a hypothesis … not measured" ([chipsets.yaml](https://github.com/Donchitos/Claude-Code-Game-Studios/blob/main/.claude/genres/chipsets.yaml)).
- **Project goal.** `/start` guesses `project.goal` (jam, learn, hobby, portfolio, sell, early_access, publisher, mobile), defaulting to `hobby`. The goal changes little: release dates in `/cut` and the note about reference IP ([effects-map.md](https://github.com/Donchitos/Claude-Code-Game-Studios/blob/main/.claude/docs/effects-map.md)).

The companion roles research found that CCGS's document-heavy path cost several times more and ranked last in blind review ([2026-11-23-game-development-roles.md](2026-11-23-game-development-roles.md)). That argues for one short brief rather than a market document set. No other agent game-dev framework checked had a research step: [abagames/agentic-gamedev-skills](https://github.com/abagames/agentic-gamedev-skills) and [HermeticOrmus/claude-code-game-development](https://github.com/HermeticOrmus/claude-code-game-development) READMEs showed none, and general "market researcher" agents exist only outside games (*search summary*). The commercial tool Ludo.ai does offer game market research: daily Steam and app-store chart trends, a "Top Charts Blender" and a "Market Analysis" that tests an idea "against similar competing games" ([Ludo.ai](https://ludo.ai/market-intelligence), *vendor, search summary*).

## Implications for GameDirector

*Everything in this section is inference from the findings above, not a cited finding.*

**Where it sits: a short research step of its own, between setup and ideation.** Ideation is a conversation that must run with the person, and the reading this work needs would fill its context. As its own session, research can run unattended, since it needs only the seed and a goal, and it fits "one step per session". A role is the wrong shape: GameDirector's roles review the running build, and market fit cannot be read from captures. Store legibility is the one part that can. "Do four captures read as this genre, and are its anchors present?" belongs in the `creative` and `ux` review lenses, not in a new role. If a new phase is too costly for the state tool, the fallback is a subagent dispatched at the start of ideation that writes the brief before step 2. That keeps `PHASES` unchanged but makes ideation wait on it.

**Setup asks one new question: what the game is for.** CCGS's goal values (sell, early access, publisher, portfolio, jam, learn, hobby) are a workable set, with hobby as the default. The goal sizes the research. Setup should also check that the market hosts are reachable. This session shows they are not, which counts as a concrete need under the environment rules.

**The brief has a fixed, small shape**, one page plus data, for example `docs/game/research/brief.md` and `comps.json`:

- the genre frame;
- 5–10 comps with retrieval date, price, top tags, review count and score, estimated units as a launch-year range, and median playtime at review;
- an anchors list, each anchor with the comps that show it;
- the genre default in one paragraph;
- complaint themes from negative reviews;
- a motivation profile (2–3 Quantic Foundry motivations, plus the SDT needs);
- platform, input, price and session norms;
- 2–3 candidate hook lines;
- reference candidates per aspect, with what to take and what to avoid;
- for a selling goal only, saturation, the odds, and the risks.

Every number carries a link and a date or says "not researched", as CCGS's rule requires. No TAM, no point estimates, and no demographics without behaviour.

**What it hands to ideation and the north stars:**

- **Ideation.**
  - *Step 2 grilling* starts from the brief. The person confirms the comps, the genre and the player rather than inventing them.
  - *Step 3 directions* each name their anchor genre, two comps and their departure. Market fit becomes a scored criterion only for a selling goal.
  - *Vision page.* "Audience and platforms" and "References" come from the person's choices.
- **North stars.**
  - *`creative`:* its question "what would most games of this kind do, and where does ours depart?" is answered by the anchors list and the genre default. The hook becomes an identity invariant.
  - *`design`:* gets the complaint themes, as candidate anti-pillars, and the comps' content and session norms.
  - *`ux`:* gets platform, input and session norms, and the four-screenshot legibility test.
  - *`art`, `audio`, `feel` and `narrative`:* get reference candidates for the person to pick from.
- **Kill condition.** For a selling goal, a market signal can join the kill condition, such as baseline daily wishlists once a store page exists. That signal needs a person and a public page, so it becomes a human-queue item after the slice verdict, never a gate before ideation.

**What stays a conversation with the person:** whether and how much the game must sell; risk appetite; which comps are "us"; which anchors to break; the hook; price and platforms; every reference pick; and any public test. The research step must not drop a direction because its numbers look weak. It states the odds, and the person chooses. This also guards the role design's "house formula" anti-goal: the brief describes the genre default so the game can depart from it on purpose, not converge on it.

**How it degrades for a hobby game.** With a hobby, jam or learn goal, skip sales estimates, saturation and price. Keep the genre anchors, because players' expectations still decide what reads as fun. Keep the references per aspect, the SDT needs, and a player profile that may simply be "my friends, who play X on phones". Draw comps from itch.io as readily as Steam. Without network access, or if the person declines the step, the brief records "not researched", ideation proceeds on the person's own references, and the step costs one short session or nothing.
