# Make Every Role Judge the Running Game

A game team divides into about nine stable ownership layers: vision (creative and game director), design (systems, level, combat, economy), look (art director, concept, environment and character, UI, animation, VFX, technical art), sound (audio director, sound designer, composer, technical sound designer, dialogue lead), story and world (narrative director, narrative designer, writer, lore keeper), player experience (UX, games user research, game feel, onboarding, accessibility), scope (producer), technology (technical director) and quality (QA lead). Each layer works from a short, recognizable set of deliverables whose best versions state rules an artifact can visibly fail: falsifiable pillars, one-page system specs, beat charts and cost curves, an art bible written after an in-engine visual target with a hex colour-role table, an audio handbook with priority tiers and a loudness target, a tone document and lean world bible, flow maps, per-verb feel targets and an onboarding plan, a scope list tested against the pitch sentence, performance budgets and a severity scheme. The stronger finding concerns review rather than paperwork: studios that sustain quality critique a playable build with people from outside the feature team, write notes that diagnose rather than prescribe, settle arguments with observed play and leave one named owner to decide, while the closest prior art to GameDirector, Claude-Code-Game-Studios, measured that its 30-document, director-gated path cost several times more and was ranked last by two blind reviewers and a human playtest. The asker's autonomous run (working on paper, bad-looking, unfun) is the documented result when completeness and stability gates are enforced and taste gates are missing or judged from text, and it is what the AI-evaluation literature predicts, because automated reviewers catch "broken" far more reliably than "good". GameDirector should therefore give each role three things: the questions it asks the person in ideation and brainstorming, one short wiki page of falsifiable rules, and a review lens that reads captures, logs and bot runs from the running build in a fresh context, measures what code can measure, and marks fun, feel, beauty and sound provisional until a person has played. One director lens owns final calls on fit, and the person's few minutes go to choosing directions and references, labelling calibration captures, playing the vertical slice and making blind A/B choices in polish.

*How far to trust the sources.* Every research note behind this report records that full-page fetching was blocked in its session (DNS or host policy), so almost all findings come from search-engine summaries of the cited pages rather than the pages themselves, and quotes are close paraphrase. Two exceptions: the Claude-Code-Game-Studios repository was read from a clone at release 1.3.0, and Anthropic's harness-design article was fetched in full. Figures a note flagged as single-source, conflicting or unverified are marked so where they appear. No studio's per-discipline review checklist, sign-off matrix or complete art or audio bible could be read, so the deliverable section lists below are the research notes' syntheses from practitioner descriptions and partial published examples, and are labelled as such.

## Nine ownership layers matter more than job titles

Job titles do not travel between studios, but ownership does. Ubisoft splits the top creative role in two: the creative director "defines and communicates the high-level creative vision" and co-owns scope with the producer ([Ubisoft CD posting](https://www.ubisoft.com/en-us/company/careers/search/744000125816642-creative-director-assassin-s-creed-)), while the game director converts that vision into mechanics and oversees "progression, pace, balance, and quality" ([Ubisoft Game Director, For Honor](https://montreal.ubisoft.com/en/jobs/game-director-for-honor/)). Some "lead designer" postings are mostly people management and others mostly hands-on design ([Built In](https://builtin.com/job/lead-game-designer/3409384)). A Game Developer art piece asks outright "who is the vision holder?" and gives the art director the job of championing it ([Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/art/a-treatise-on-video-game-art---what-is-the-formula-for-success-)), and Ubisoft's own narrative designers say "ask 10 narrative designers to describe their job, and you will get 10 different answers" ([Ubisoft News](https://news.ubisoft.com/en-us/article/7m412GLSbfkaT0YheRYLVG/what-is-narrative-design)). **Naughty Dog runs with no dedicated producers**, perhaps two production coordinators for about 300 people against a common ratio of one producer per ten developers, so its leads absorb the work ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/dice-2010-naughty-dog-s-lemarchand-on-why-the-studio-has-no-producers); [LA Times](https://www.pressreader.com/usa/los-angeles-times/20160528/282084866051373)). The durable unit is the set of decisions a role owns and the lens it judges with, summarized below from postings, practitioner sources and the notes' synthesis.

| Layer | Roles | Owns (final say) | Judges work by |
|---|---|---|---|
| Vision | Creative director; game director or lead designer | Why the game exists and what it feels like: vision, player fantasy, pillars and anti-pillars, tone, references; the feature set and its aggregate outcome | Pillar fit; whether the intended experience appears in observed play; distinctiveness |
| Design | Systems, level/mission, combat/gameplay, economy/balance designers | Rules and data; space and sequence; verbs and opposition; quantities over time | Observed play (deaths, paths, time per beat); tell and role clarity; cost curves and source/sink ratios |
| Look | Art director; concept, environment, character and UI artists; animator; VFX artist; technical artist | Visual identity end to end; exploration; production to spec; UI visual language; motion; effects as gameplay signal; pipeline, shaders and budgets | Match to a visual target and art bible; value, silhouette, colour roles, noise, cohesion |
| Sound | Audio director; sound designer; composer; technical sound designer; voice/dialogue lead | Sonic identity, guidelines, pipeline and final mix; effects; music; middleware and runtime budgets; scripts and recording | Can players read the game by ear; feedback coverage; mix priority; loudness; repetition |
| Story and world | Narrative director or lead writer; narrative designer; writer; world builder or lore lead | Story vision and narrative budget; delivery through systems and spaces; the words; canon and continuity | Theme and tone; ludonarrative harmony; character voice; continuity; originality |
| Player experience | UX designer; games user researcher; feel owner; onboarding; accessibility | How the player perceives, understands and operates the game; player evidence; feel; the first minutes; access | Heuristics plus observed players; latency and feedback; tutorial funnel; guideline checks |
| Scope | Producer | Feature list against the pitch, milestones, cuts | Must-list against remaining time; testable acceptance criteria |
| Technology | Technical director | Performance budgets, pipeline and tools, build stability, certification readiness | Profiling against budget at every stage |
| Quality | QA lead | Test plans, severity and priority scheme, exit thresholds, compliance checklist | Crash rate, blockers, regressions |

Four overlaps need an explicit owner in any agent system: combat math sits between systems and combat design, encounter spaces between level and combat design, progression between systems and economy, and vision itself when both a creative and a game director exist. Game feel is the widest overlap, spanning design tuning, animation, VFX, audio, camera and engineering, and the player-experience note recommends a single feel owner with a veto over "done" on core actions. Colour is shared as well, since the art bible's colour roles feed UI palettes, VFX bands and faction palettes. Small teams merge layers rather than drop them: Greg Kasavin describes his Hades job as "writing and narrative design" ([National Centre for Writing](https://nationalcentreforwriting.org.uk/writing-hub/writing-hades-with-greg-kasavin/)), audio director, sound designer and technical sound designer are often one person, and Hollow Knight's look is credited mainly to one artist ([BrushBit](https://agfrag.com/research/articles/art-of-indie-game-design/)). In an agent system the layers become review lenses rather than staff, and the evidence says a lens earns its place through the criteria and inputs it carries, not its persona label: across 162 personas and 2,410 factual questions, persona prompts gave no reliable benefit over no persona, and picking the best persona automatically was no better than random ([Zheng et al., EMNLP Findings 2024](https://aclanthology.org/2024.findings-emnlp.888/)).

## Every discipline writes a short direction that an artifact can fail

The disciplines follow one arc. Each starts with a short verbal north star, gathers references, explores divergent options, builds one target at intended quality, writes down only the rules that target proved, lists the content to make, and reviews new work against the target. The deliverables that work share a property: every rule is one a concrete asset, scene or build could visibly fail. A survey of public art bibles asks for visual goals "phrased so an asset could actually fail them" and a tie-breaker for disputes ([Makko](https://blog.makko.ai/what-public-game-art-bibles-say/)), and CCGS's pillar gate asks whether "a real design decision [could] actually fail this pillar" ([cd-pillars.md](https://github.com/Donchitos/Claude-Code-Game-Studios/blob/main/.claude/docs/director-gates/cd-pillars.md)). Length has a poor record by comparison. Stone Librande built his one-page design method on the observation that readers rarely get past the first page ([GDC Vault](https://gdcvault.com/play/1012785/One-Page)), and Deus Ex's design document grew from **64 to about 500 pages with "radically different" content** by its first alpha ([Vice](https://www.vice.com/en/article/the-original-deus-ex-design-document-is-fascinating-reading)).

### Creative and game direction: a vision packet used as a cut filter

The vision holder produces a small packet whose job is to give every later decision a filter. Game Developer's "Establishing Vision" splits creative direction into vision, core pillars, core loop and fantasy, insisting that "a vision is the destination, not the journey" ([Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/establishing-vision)). Pillars are either brainstormed by the team or proposed by the vision holder and presented ([Wireframe](https://www.raspberrypi.com/news/how-pillars-and-triangles-can-focus-your-game-design/)), and an informal guide recommends three to five to avoid diluting focus ([mehendysis](https://mehendysis.github.io/GameDevelopment/GDD/game-design-direction/design-pillars)). Schell's Lens of the Essential Experience supplies the core questions: what experience the player should have, what is essential to it, and how the game captures that essence ([NotesByLex](https://notesbylex.com/the-art-of-game-design-a-book-of-lenses-2nd-edition-by-jesse-schell)). MDA lets the director rank target aesthetics such as Sensation, Challenge, Discovery or Expression, from a list its authors call non-exhaustive ([Hunicke, LeBlanc & Zubek](https://users.cs.northwestern.edu/%7Ehunicke/MDA.pdf)). The pitch documents are Rogers' one-sheet and "ten-pager", the latter more a set of guidelines than a strict page limit ([O'Reilly, Level Up!](https://www.oreilly.com/library/view/level-up-the/9780470688670/ch19.html)); the one-sheet's exact field list was not verified. The packet ends in a vertical slice definition, which sources describe as a compact playable cross-section of roughly 20 minutes to an hour (single source) and distinguish from a prototype, which only tests whether an idea is fun ([Tono Game Consultants](https://tonogameconsultants.com/vertical-slice/); [YuvalGG](https://yuvalgg.com/game-development/what-is-a-vertical-slice)).

| Deliverable | Contents |
|---|---|
| Vision statement | One paragraph on who the player is, what they do and what they feel, plus Schell's three essential-experience answers |
| Player fantasy | One second-person sentence ("You are…") |
| Pillars and anti-pillars | 3–5 pillars, each a claim a playtest could falsify, with one feature it admits and one it rejects; anti-pillars naming what the game will not be |
| Target aesthetics | 2–3 MDA aesthetics, ranked |
| Core loop | Verb → challenge → reward → investment, plus the meta loop it feeds; loops for mastery, arcs for authored content |
| Reference set | Games and other media, each with the element borrowed and the deliberate difference (the hook) |
| One-sheet and ten-pager outline | Pitch summary; the ten-pager's page list (story, characters and controls, gameplay, mechanics, enemies, bonus content, monetization) doubles as a brainstorming agenda after genre substitution |
| Vertical slice definition | Which loop, space and systems, and what "proven" means, written as pillar claims |

These contents are the design note's synthesis from the sources above; the "falsifiable pillar" rule comes from low-authority AI-skill prior art rather than studio practice.

The director reviews by filtering. Pillars prevent "gluing on more and more mechanics that lose focus and cause delays" ([Game Design Skills](https://gamedesignskills.com/game-design/design-pillars/)), but the filter keeps as well as cuts: God of War (2018) kept puzzles, which were not a pillar, because they served several pillars at once ([God of War wiki, GDC talk summary](https://godofwar.fandom.com/wiki/GDC_%22The_Level_Design_of_God_of_War%22)). Santa Monica handled David Jaffe's late requests with a rate limit, "if David asked for it three times he probably really wants it", then implemented the survivors by impact ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/gdc-i-god-of-war-i-how-the-left-and-right-brain-learned-to-love-one-another)). The documented failure modes are feature creep, more pillars than anyone can hold, pillar wording that drifts (the same God of War pillars appear as "combat, narrative, exploration" in one account and "Combat, Father/Son, Exploration" in another ([PlayStation Blog](https://blog.playstation.com/2018/12/05/how-santa-monica-studios-nailed-exploration-in-god-of-war/))), documents drifting from the build, and consensus groups that bottleneck: Valve's Cabal, a success on Half-Life, became a bottleneck on Half-Life 2 ([Valve HL2 slides](https://www.gamedevs.org/uploads/valve-design-process-for-creating-half-life2.ppt)). The vision review's central question is Schell's gap check, where a large difference between intended and observed experience means the game must change, and it only works on observed play.

### Design specialists: one-page specs, beat charts, rosters and cost curves

Systems designers own rules and data: specs for mechanics and their tunables, plus what Insomniac's lead systems posting calls "standards and metrics" that keep systems consistent across the game ([Insomniac posting](https://job-boards.greenhouse.io/insomniac/jobs/6009623004)). Daniel Cook's skill atoms give a spec unit, action → simulation → feedback, with new skills built on mastered ones ([AAAI paper](https://cdn.aaai.org/ojs/13040/13040-52-16557-1-2-20201228.pdf)), and his loops-versus-arcs split separates repeated mastery from content consumed once ([Mechanics of Magic](https://mechanicsofmagic.com/2022/05/04/sketchnote-loops-and-arcs-2/)). Level designers own space and sequence through a short, physical process: a "metrics zoo" test map for player physics such as speed, step height and jump distance ([Level Design Book, Metrics](https://book.leveldesignbook.com/process/blockout/metrics)); a beat chart that proves pacing on paper before geometry, with a retune log after each playtest ([allo.io](https://allo.io/blog/en/level-design-document-template/)); and a blockout, because "you can't playtest a design document or a layout sketch, but you can playtest a blockout", and "99% of the time, your blockout will not survive a playtest" ([Level Design Book, Blockout](https://book.leveldesignbook.com/process/blockout)). Combat designers own the roster and encounters. Each enemy needs a silhouette readable at mid-range (about 10 m or more) and animations that telegraph state and intent ([Level Design Book, Enemy design](https://book.leveldesignbook.com/process/combat/enemy)), a tell long enough to perceive and respond to but not so long that combat feels sluggish ([Bugnet](https://bugnet.io/blog/how-to-design-enemy-attack-telegraphs)), and one distinct pressure that combines with others in an encounter ([Abratabia](https://www.abratabia.com/game-ai-npc/enemy-ai-patterns.php)). Economy designers own quantities over time: list and tally every source and sink, where one spreadsheet suffices for a fixed-length game ([Lost Garden](https://lostgarden.com/2021/12/12/value-chains/)); design sinks before entering balance values ([Sarvotam](https://sarvotamsolutions.com/blog-liveops-economy-currencies-sinks-faucets/)); and plot options on a cost curve, where "overpowered" means "undercosted" ([course notes on Schreiber](https://bowling-bash.blogspot.com/2011/03/literature-game-balance-ch3-transitive.html)). One indie source sets faucets at 1.1–1.3× sinks per tier (single source) ([StraySpark](https://www.strayspark.studio/blog/game-economy-balancing-spreadsheets)).

| Role | Core deliverables and their contents |
|---|---|
| Systems designer | One page per system: purpose and pillar served, player verbs, rules and states, inputs and outputs to other systems, tunables with defaults, skill atoms taught, edge cases, test cases; one global standards-and-metrics sheet |
| Level / mission designer | Metrics zoo; per level a goal and pillar, beat chart (time-ordered beats with intensity, mechanic introduced or tested, reward), top-down sketch, blockout, critical-path and reachability check, retune log |
| Combat / gameplay designer | Verb list with timing and feel targets; enemy roster (role, silhouette, telegraph, counter, pressure applied); encounter templates combining roles; difficulty ramp across beats |
| Economy / balance designer | Resource list; source/sink map per value chain; cost curve; reward schedule; difficulty curve; archetype simulations (fast vs slow progression, high vs low spender); tuning tables |

The table is the design note's synthesis; a distinct mission-designer process and Naughty Dog's Uncharted 2 encounter pipeline could not be verified.

Design work is reviewed in play. Valve recruited local players and had a staffer sit behind each one, forbidden to say anything beyond starting the game or resetting it after a crash, across **200-plus Half-Life sessions** ([Game Developer, The Cabal](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/the-cabal-valve-s-design-process-for-creating-i-half-life-i-)), and on Half-Life 2 "continued to use them as a way to settle design arguments" ([Half-Life 2 postmortem](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/classic-postmortem-the-making-of-i-half-life-2-i-)). Level reviews record deaths, paths, completion times and missed content; one template targets beat-chart timing within about 20% of observed timing (single-source example) ([allo.io](https://allo.io/blog/en/level-design-document-template/)). Combat reviews check that every enemy has a tell, a role, a counter and a distinct silhouette, and apply the death-replay test, which passes when a player watching their death says "oh, I see what I should have done" ([Choost Games](https://choostgames.com/blog/what-makes-a-good-boss-fight/)). The classic frameworks are most useful to an agent as one review question each:

| Framework | Review question it contributes |
|---|---|
| MDA | Which target aesthetic does each mechanic's dynamic actually produce in play? |
| Schell | What experience was intended, what did players have, and what caused the gap? |
| Meier | Is each decision in the loop a real trade-off with no dominant answer? (fits strategy best) |
| Koster | What is the player learning now, and if bored, is the pattern absent, revealed too fast or too slow, or mastered? |
| Chen | Can weaker and stronger players each stay in their own flow zone? |
| Hodent | Can a new player perceive, understand and act, and do they want to continue? |
| Cook | Is each skill atom's feedback clear, and does a loop build mastery while an arc delivers meaning? |
| Schreiber | Is any option undercosted, and is the matchup structure transitive or intransitive by intent? |

The questions are the design note's synthesis from [MDA](https://users.cs.northwestern.edu/%7Ehunicke/MDA.pdf), [Schell](https://notesbylex.com/the-art-of-game-design-a-book-of-lenses-2nd-edition-by-jesse-schell), [Meier's GDC 2012 talk](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/gdc-2012-sid-meier-on-how-to-see-games-as-sets-of-interesting-decisions), [Koster](https://www.shortform.com/blog/what-is-the-meaning-of-fun/), [Chen](https://cacmb4.acm.org/magazines/2007/4/5692-flow-in-games-and-everything-else/fulltext), [Hodent](https://www.pocketgamer.biz/celia-hodent-on-the-importance-of-cognitive-science-and-a-ux-framework-to-improve-your-game), [Cook](https://cdn.aaai.org/ojs/13040/13040-52-16557-1-2-20201228.pdf) and [Schreiber](https://bowling-bash.blogspot.com/2011/03/literature-game-balance-ch3-transitive.html).

### Art: from slogan and references to a visual target, then a falsifiable art bible

Published art-direction cases follow the same order. A verbal north star comes first: Breath of the Wild's look was set by the slogan "refreshing and full-flavored" ([Nintendo Everything](https://nintendoeverything.com/zelda-breath-of-the-wild-art-director-on-how-the-wind-waker-hd-shaped-the-games-art-style/)), Sea of Thieves' art director described "a few simple rules" that permeate all of a game's visuals ([80.lv](https://80.lv/articles/gdc18-visual-adventures-on-sea-of-thieves)), and Overwatch's values included "big, bold silhouettes" and handcrafting everything so the world "felt made by people rather than simulated by computers" ([Vice](https://www.vice.com/en/article/heres-what-its-like-to-art-direct-overwatch/); [Cook & Becker](https://www.cookandbecker.com/en/article/378/designing-overwatch.html)). These pillars serve gameplay and business constraints, not taste alone. Valorant's style aimed at hardware reach and art that "complemented but did not overshadow the gameplay" ([Inverse](https://www.inverse.com/gaming/valorant-art-style-interview-moby-francke)), and Team Fortress 2's stylization served readability ([Valve GDC 2008 slides](https://cdn.cloudflare.steamstatic.com/apps/valve/2008/GDC2008_StylizationWithAPurpose_TF2.pdf)). References come next and name a tradition: TF2 drew on Leyendecker and Rockwell, Hades on classical art ([Wikipedia, Jen Zee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jen_Zee)). Exploration runs wide then narrows, with practitioner rules of thumb of 20–50 thumbnails per major piece and 3–8 iterations before approval (practitioner figures, not studio data) ([DreamPixelForge](https://www.dreampixelforge.com/blog/how-to-make-concept-art)). Then comes a built visual target. Overwatch's team said "we used Torbjorn as our art visual target, and he was the first one we modeled" ([Kotaku](https://kotaku.com/without-torbjorn-overwatch-wouldnt-be-overwatch-1792919785)); other teams build a "beauty corner", a small and usually unplayable area showing the final look, "not too far from a Vertical Slice, but focused on the art and level design pipeline" ([GDKeys](https://gdkeys.com/game-development-process/)). Only after that is the art bible written, followed by the asset list.

| Deliverable | Contents |
|---|---|
| Visual north star | A one-line slogan plus 3–5 visual pillars phrased as rules ("big, bold silhouettes", "no granular texture noise"), each naming what it rules out and the game pillar or constraint it serves |
| References and mood boards | Named traditions inside and outside games, sorted by purpose (shape, palette, lighting, material, UI) rather than by mood alone |
| Style directions | 3–5 divergent directions, each a different reference mix with its own "X, never Y" rules; one chosen, with the reasons the others died recorded |
| Visual target | One hero asset and one beauty corner or target screenshot at final quality in the real engine, proving the style is buildable and setting cost per asset |
| Art bible | Ownership and version; visual target with tie-breaker; pillars with "fails if" tests; camera and projection; scale chart by asset class; shape language; colour-role table with hex values; value structure; lighting rules (key direction, shadow colour and opacity, warm/cool shift); materials and texel or pixel density; character, environment, prop, UI and VFX sub-rules; budgets; naming and folders |
| Colour palette | A role table rather than a swatch: reserved hues for self/friendly, threat, interactable and pickup, with environment palettes kept out of them; value and saturation bands per layer; for small teams a fixed palette (a 16-colour start is common) with a role per colour |
| Concept handoff package | Orthographic model sheet at correct proportion, colour and material callout, low-resolution silhouette test, paint-over reference; turnaround and expression sheets for characters |
| UI style guide and HUD spec | A "single source of truth" for palette by function, type scale and minimum sizes, icon grammar, button kit with states, layout grid and safe areas, and motion timing; then a complete HUD spec before UI art production |
| Animation style guide | Snappy or deliberate; anticipation policy (minimal for the player, readable and scaled to threat for enemies); cancel and regain-control frames; exaggeration level; critical-path tier list (locomotion, core actions, death) |
| VFX style guide | Riot's model: goals (clarity, minimal clutter, theme, delight); levers (gameplay, value, colour, shape, timing); a primary element carrying the gameplay signal and a secondary carrying theme; anticipation–main–dissipation timing; clutter and performance budgets |
| Technical-art budgets and enforcement | Polygon, texture, bone and particle budgets; naming and import validation; shaders that enforce the style (palette quantize, outline, fixed lighting rig) |

The art bible minimum comes from Makko's survey of public bibles ([Makko](https://blog.makko.ai/what-public-game-art-bibles-say/); [template](https://blog.makko.ai/game-art-bible-template/)) and recurring sections from [GameDev.net](https://gamedev.net/forums/topic/552212-creating-art-biblestyle-guide/); the concept package from [Game-Ace](https://game-ace.com/blog/video-game-concept-art/); the UI guide from [Sunstrike](https://sunstrikestudios.com/en/blog/what-is-game-ui/) and [Nasty Rodent](https://nastyrodent.com/what-is-hud-in-games/); the animation guide from [MocapOnline](https://mocaponline.com/blogs/mocap-news/game-animation-documentation-guide) and [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/production/the-12-principles-of-animation-in-video-games); the VFX guide from [gdad.wiki](https://gdad.wiki/wiki/sources/source-lol-vfx-style-guide) and a [Riot mirror](https://lol.garena.com/news/general/league%E2%80%99s-vfx-style-guide); budgets from [Tech-Artists.Org](https://techartorg.github.io/ta-101/job-descriptions/job-descriptions-games/). Several of these are vendor blogs, and the full section lists are the art note's synthesis because no complete AAA bible was readable.

The most concrete public guide is the Liberated Pixel Cup style guide for 2D. It fixes a camera about 60° from top-down rendered orthographically with perspective tricks disallowed, light from the top slightly front-left, shading that hue-shifts toward purple in shadow and yellow in highlight, characters on their own palettes so they stand out from the background, and drop shadows in one hex colour ([LPC style guide](https://lpc.opengameart.org/static/LPC-Style-Guide/build/styleguide.html)). Versions conflict on that colour, #322125 at 60% opacity in the OpenGameArt guide and #2a1722 at alpha 120/256 in the revised wiki ([LPC Revised wiki](https://github.com/ElizaWy/LPC/wiki/Style-Guide)). 0 A.D.'s art document caps rigs at 25 bones and assigns texture sizes by asset tier, and is reported as 21 sections by one review and thirty by another (unverified) ([Makko](https://blog.makko.ai/what-public-game-art-bibles-say/)). Readability rules agree across studios. TF2's silhouettes must read at first glance, the read order is "Run or Attack?" then "What's he packin'?", and value grades from dark feet to a light chest where the weapon sits ([Valve slides](https://cdn.cloudflare.steamstatic.com/apps/valve/2008/GDC2008_StylizationWithAPurpose_TF2.pdf)). Riot's VFX guide puts UI, characters, VFX and environment in separate value and saturation bands and keeps effects away from pure 0% and 100% ([gdad.wiki](https://gdad.wiki/wiki/sources/source-lol-vfx-style-guide)), and Riot's clarity rule says the most important thing at a moment must draw the most attention because "visual noise adds up quickly" ([Clarity in League](https://www.leagueoflegends.com/en-us/news/dev/clarity-in-league/)). Overwatch revised Mei's silhouette when it resembled Zarya's ([Wikipedia, Mei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mei_(Overwatch))). Xbox Accessibility Guideline 103 forbids colour as the only carrier of information and edits its own example screenshots with colour-blindness simulators ([XAG 103](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/xbox/accessibility/xbox-accessibility-guidelines/103)).

Art reviews happen on screenshots of the build. The standard correction tool is the paint-over, where an artist paints on a screenshot to fix proportion, silhouette, palette and framing ([Game-Ace](https://game-ace.com/blog/video-game-concept-art/)). Feedback states what is wrong, why it is a problem and where to go next, tied to the goal rather than to feelings ([Vsquad](https://vsquad.art/blog/expert-interviews/danila-vlasov-art-direction-interview/)). One art lead's checklist covers base colour, roughness, metallicity, black-and-white contrast, match to reference, depth, scale and colour harmony ([Sergei Panin](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/sergei-panin-131ba769_artfeedback-gamedev-activity-7167826698627977216-tn-W)); composition reviews check focal hierarchy, quiet areas, grouping, flow and weight ([Nasty Rodent](https://nastyrodent.com/concept-art-composition/)); and HUDs are judged in-engine under real lighting, motion and VFX rather than as isolated screens ([Argentics](https://www.argentics.io/game-hud-design)). The commonest failure is incoherence. Researchers tie "asset flips", with mismatched aesthetics and incoherent style, to missing art direction ([Prefabbashing](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/334120358_Prefabbashing_-_The_Flip_Side_of_Asset_Stores)); one developer admitted "the pixel art was supposed to be placeholder but stayed in due to time constraints" ([itch.io](https://itch.io/post/6982665)); and practitioners argue a rough but coherent direction reads as intentional while mixed high-fidelity assets look cheap ([Bugnet](https://bugnet.io/blog/art-direction-beats-art-budget)). Distinctiveness is protected two ways. Directors cut what breaks the style even when it is fun, as Sword of the Sea removed grind rails because they "did not fit the style" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sword_of_the_Sea)), and they name identity invariants, as Overwatch 2 kept Tracer's palette, silhouette and sense of motion through her redesign ([Blizzard](https://overwatch.blizzard.com/en-gb/news/23189038/revving-up-the-engine-overwatch-2-evolving-the-art-panel-recap/)). Hades shows the signature-motif habit, with Hades' beard shaped as the Sigil of the Dead ([Point'n Think](https://www.pointnthink.fr/en/the-art-of-hades-en/)). For small teams the sources favour constraint over fidelity: realistic environments usually look worse for solo developers than a deliberate stylized approach ([SJHYPS](https://shjhyps.com/articles/article-art-direction-indie.html)), A Short Hike keeps textures flat with a custom shader ([Uniday](https://uniday.studio/blog/9-the-art-of-indie-game-design-crafting-visual-delight-with-limited-resources)), and sourced assets must be normalized to the house style or "you end up with conflicting art styles" ([itch.io](https://itch.io/post/4377534)).

### Audio: pillars, a priority model and a loudness spec

The audio director owns the audio vision, the guidelines, the pipeline and, in several postings, "the final mix and overall sonic polish" ([Berklee](https://www.berklee.edu/careers/roles/audio-director); [Absurd Ventures](https://job-boards.greenhouse.io/absurdventures/jobs/4904230007)). The clearest audio vision on record is Overwatch's "Play by Sound", a directive from the game director that turned the mix into an information system ranking each enemy's importance by threat, gaze, fire and distance ([GDC Vault](https://gdcvault.com/play/1023010/Overwatch-The-Elusive-Goal-Play); [PC Gamer](https://pcgamer.com/how-overwatch-uses-sound-to-pinpoint-threats-you-cant-see)). Process runs from pillars to vocabulary to bible: first one or two style pillars of a couple of sentences each, often tied to the visual style; then a "common vocabulary" of references from films, games, books and music; then a style bible, or a lighter "style handbook", that covers effects, voice and music and is not so rigid that parts cannot be replaced ([Designing Sound, 2012](https://designingsound.org/2012/02/07/wrangling-aesthetic/)). A Sound Effect questions whether huge feature-by-feature audio documents get read at all ([A Sound Effect](https://asoundeffect.com/game-audio-design-document/)). Composers "spot" a game by playing early builds and sitting in brainstorms, and the named failure is a composer handed a fixed cue list with only screenshots or video who never plays the game ([Audiokinetic](https://audiokinetic.com/the-business-of-music-design-for-games-and-dynamic-storytelling)). Adaptive music plans choose among horizontal resequencing, vertical layering and hybrids such as Sackboy's success melody layered over horizontal segments ([Phillips, GDC 2021](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/hybrid-horizontal-vertical-structure-for-game-music-composers-from-spyder-to-sackboy-gdc-2021-)). Dialogue leads keep a script database with stable, non-positional line IDs and flagged placeholders ([wildwinter](https://wildwinter.medium.com/a-dialogue-pipeline-db0be8d8509c)), prototype with text-to-speech ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/prototyping-dialogue-with-google-text-to-speech)), and treat the voice director as "the last quality check" on takes that must survive repetition ([Audio Gang](https://www.audiogang.org/?p=2463)).

| Audio bible section | Contents |
|---|---|
| Audio pillars | 1–3 statements of two sentences or fewer, each tied to a game pillar, with a "this, not that" pair |
| Role of audio | What the player must be able to do by ear; which information is audio-first |
| Reference vocabulary | 5–15 references, each with the one quality borrowed, plus anti-references |
| Sonic palette per category | Player, threats, world, UI, music, voice: sources (recorded, synthesized, library, generated), timbre and frequency zone, a fixed processing chain, "never" rules |
| Signature sounds | The 3–7 sounds that define the game, custom or heavily transformed |
| Priority model | Tiers (player-critical information, own-action feedback, others' actions, ambience and music), ducking rules, voice limits per tier |
| Music direction | Role of music, instrumentation, themes, adaptive scheme, state map, transitions, silence policy |
| Voice direction | Scope (full, barks, gibberish, none), character voice sheets, line-ID scheme, placeholder/TTS policy |
| UI sound family | Shared key, timbre and envelope for confirm, cancel, navigate, error, reward |
| Mix and loudness spec | Integrated target and tolerance per platform, true-peak ceiling, bus structure, dialogue anchor |
| Technical spec | Format, naming, loop rules, variation minimums, memory and voice budgets |
| Accessibility | Per-category volume sliders, mono, subtitles and captions, a visual cue for every critical sound |

The section list is the audio note's synthesis from Designing Sound, mix-priority guidance that orders dialogue, then gameplay-critical cues, then ambience and music ([Bugnet](https://bugnet.io/blog/how-to-mix-game-audio-so-nothing-drowns-out)), and the accessibility guidelines; no real studio audio bible was readable.

Mix targets cite platform loudness standards measured with ITU-R BS.1770 ([ITU](https://www.itu.int/dms_pubrec/itu-r/rec/bs/R-REC-BS.1770-5-202311-I!!PDF-E.pdf)): **−24 LUFS ±2 with a −1 dBTP true-peak ceiling for console**, per GANG IESD and Sony's ASWG-R001 as reported secondhand ([GANG IESD](https://www.audiogang.org/wp-content/uploads/2015/04/IESD-Mix-Ref-Levels-v03.02.pdf); [VoxBooster](https://voxbooster.com/blog/game-loudness-lufs-compliance-statistics-2026/)). Mobile targets conflict, −16 LUFS from GANG against −18 LKFS ±2 reported for Sony portable. Audiokinetic cites 30–70 physical voices as a typical runtime budget ([Audiokinetic](https://www.audiokinetic.com/wwise-cpu-optimizations-general-guidelines)). Distinctiveness, according to mostly vendor sources, comes from spending custom work on the few signature sounds players hear most and using libraries for the rest, because "the magic of sound design is not the source; it's the manipulation" ([SFX Engine](https://sfxengine.com/blog/sound-design-for-games)); the triage question is one audio director's "Would the player care?" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/audio/10-tips-the-creation-and-integration-of-audio)). Variation should change texture without erasing the cue that identifies a sound, as PlanetSide 2's audio director warned ([GameSpot](https://gamespot.com/articles/the-state-of-the-mmo-creating-a-soundscape-part-2/1100-6405824/)). Reviews follow fixed passes: a deliberate missing- and broken-sound sweep, because visually focused testers miss them, and balance tests in real combined situations, because a mix can fail when every sound is fine alone ([Bugnet](https://bugnet.io/blog/qa-testing-for-game-audio-and-mixing)); pre-mixes of dialogue, music, in-game effects and cutscenes feeding a final mix, with a dialogue "pre-pre-mix" on Prototype 2 ([Rob Bridgett](https://gamedeveloper.com/audio/notes-from-the-mix-i-prototype-2-i-)); a final mix on calibrated speakers, at 75 dB SPL on Tell Me Why ([Audiokinetic](https://audiokinetic.com/en/blog/tell-me-why-audio-diary-part-4-mixing-and-mastering)); and an accessibility pass covering subtitles for speech and captions for all audio including speaker and direction ([XAG 104](https://devdocs.xbox.com/gaming/accessibility/xbox-accessibility-guidelines/104)), plus separate volume controls and a mono option, with Microsoft's warning that collapsing to mono can make important cues harder to tell apart ([XAG 105](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/gaming/accessibility/xbox-accessibility-guidelines/105)). The stakes are not cosmetic: a poor mix can "even make the game unplayable" ([Alex Riviere](https://guava-gold-ceet.squarespace.com/knowledgebase/how-to-mix-and-qa-test-your-game)).

### Narrative and world: a tone document, a lean bible, and story carried by play

Narrative splits four ways even where titles merge. Writers own the words (dialogue, item descriptions, lore) while narrative designers own how story reaches the player through actions and systems ([Medium](https://medium.com/teeny-tiny-game-dev-essays/the-difference-between-narrative-designers-game-writers-ffcb50de9efe)); Ubisoft's narrative designers call their work "mostly about wielding game systems" ([Ubisoft News](https://news.ubisoft.com/en-us/article/7m412GLSbfkaT0YheRYLVG/what-is-narrative-design)), and a PlayStation lead narrative designer posting asks for "architecting and designing the narrative systems which realize the Game Directors' vision" ([Hitmarker](https://hitmarker.net/jobs/playstation-lead-narrative-designer-404383)). A narrative director oversees narrative in large studios, while small teams merge designer and writer ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Narrative_designer)). No source defined a separate world-builder or lore-lead title, so that charter (canon, timeline, factions, naming, glossary, continuity) is inferred; Disco Elysium shows it held by the lead writer, with a setting carrying more than 6,000 years of history from tabletop campaigns and an explicit organizing idea in Hegelian dialectics ([Wikipedia, Robert Kurvitz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Kurvitz)). Sources agree there is no standard template and that bibles should be modular, only as deep as play needs, and edited "over and over until the very end of production" ([Blind Squirrel](https://blindsquirrelentertainment.com/news/Narrative-Design-Part-3); [River Editor](https://rivereditor.com/blogs/build-fantasy-world-bible-7-days-2026)). Narrative pillars should complement design pillars, and tone informs "every aspect of game development, from narrative to art to music to design" ([Saltstone Studios](https://saltstonestudios.com/2025/12/08/game-design-pillars/)).

| Deliverable | Contents |
|---|---|
| Narrative one-pager | Logline, player fantasy, theme as a question, tone (three words and three "not" words), setting in one sentence, protagonist's want, need and flaw, central conflict, ending shape, delivery modes, scope (words, voice lines, cutscenes), comparables and the twist |
| Narrative pillars | 3–5, each with "this means / this doesn't mean", one gameplay expression, one sample line, and the design pillar it supports |
| Tone document | Tone words and anti-words; sliders (comic–grave, grounded–fantastical, sincere–ironic); touchstones per discipline; 5–10 "this, not that" pairs; where tone may break; content boundaries |
| World bible | Overview and twist; timeline; locations with their role in play; cultures and factions; rules of magic or technology with costs and limits; naming; secrets and reveal order; glossary; canon status and change log per entry |
| Character sheet | Story and gameplay role; want, need, fear, secret; arc; voice (diction, tics, banned words, sample lines and barks); visual notes for art; casting notes; what they know per chapter |
| Beat sheet and quest doc | Per beat: location, player goal, information delivered, emotional target, delivery method, flags, line estimate, critical or optional; per quest: hook, steps, branches, fail states, rewards, journal text |
| Style guide, naming and glossary | UI and codex voice, capitalization, string-length limits, localization rules; per-culture phonology and a ban list of stock genre words; terms with pronunciation and do-not-translate flags |
| Minimum kit for story-light games | Fantasy line, setting with one unexpected detail, theme phrase, tone rules with sample strings, personality cards for every recurring face, a UI voice guide, one naming scheme, and what failure, scoring and progress mean in the fiction |

The section lists are the narrative note's synthesis; the glossary, factions, notable people and change-log items are backed by the cited bible sources.

Integration is where narrative bears most on fun. Clint Hocking coined "ludonarrative dissonance" in 2007 for BioShock's mechanics rewarding self-interest while its story preached selflessness ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ludonarrative_dissonance)). Harvey Smith and Matthias Worch defined environmental storytelling as "staging player-space with environmental properties that can be interpreted as a meaningful whole" ([GDC Vault](https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1012647)). Hades builds story into its death loop, since dying returns the player to a hub where dialogue advances, and Kasavin worked with audio director Darren Korb from the earliest stages while recording dialogue continuously for about three years ([ScreenHub](https://www.screenhub.com.au/news-article/features/digital/jini-maxwell/hades-greg-kasavin-breaks-down-supergiants-unique-approach-to-narrative-262459); [GeekDad](https://geekdad.com/2019/10/narrative-and-early-access-supergiants-greg-kasavin-discusses-hades-development/)), reaching about 21,000 voice lines by his reported count ([GameRant](https://gamerant.com/hades-word-count-illiad-odyssey/)). Barks deserve attention because they are "the stuff that players are going to be hearing the most often in the game" ([Richard Dansky, Kotaku](https://kotaku.com/why-video-game-characters-say-such-ridiculous-things-5921878)), and over-explaining is "one of the cardinal sins of game dialogue" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/8-key-principles-of-writing-effective-game-dialogue)). Narrative review is continuous: table reads and peer review before scripts go to editing, localization and voice ([SIE](https://sie.com/en/blog/writing-for-video-games-part-2-the-process-and-advice-professionals-wish-they-received)); dedicated editors for consistency on large branching projects such as Fallout 4 ([Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/audio/game-editing-demystified)); continuity, tone, ludonarrative, bark and localization checks, with expansion allowances of about +30% for German (single source) ([IF craft corpus](https://pvliesdonk.github.io/if-craft-corpus/corpus-docs/audience-and-access/localization_considerations)); and voice sessions booked at least twice, with any booth changes re-reviewed ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/blogs/how-to-get-the-most-out-of-your-voice-actors)). Originality reviews demand specificity, since "a platter of meat is far less evocative" than a named dish ([Writers Helping Writers](https://writershelpingwriters.net/2022/10/the-top-three-world-building-pitfalls-and-how-to-avoid-them/)), and guard against "Fantasyland", where every trope appears "without any peculiar twist" ([Novecento](https://novecento.substack.com/p/5-pitfalls-to-avoid-when-making-an)), and "Worldbuilder's Disease", detail no player needs ([World Anvil](https://academy.worldanvil.com/blog/avoiding-worldbuilding-mistakes)). The narrative note turns these into a swap test (could this name, line or faction drop into another game unchanged?), a twist test, a point-of-view test and a need test (does this entry change what the player sees, hears, reads or does?). Indie experience adds a cost lesson: one developer wrote more than 30,000 words before reworking the concept and wished they had tested the premise first ([Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/making-story-driven-indie-games-with-no-budget)).

### Player experience: flows, feel sheets, first minutes and access

Game UX owns whether the fun is reachable. Celia Hodent's framework judges usability (perception, attention, memory) and "engage-ability" (motivation, emotion, game flow) ([EGD News](https://elitegamedevelopers.substack.com/p/egd-news-37-motivations-to-play)), and games keep intended friction, the challenge, while removing unintended friction ([IxDF](https://ixdf.org/literature/article/design-for-joy-why-games-break-traditional-ux-rules)). The deliverables are a target-player profile, flow maps, wireframes and greybox UI, an input map, and a HUD built as information inventory → categorization → layout zones → dynamic behaviours ([UXPin](https://www.uxpin.com/studio/blog/game-ux/); [CCGS ux-design via Tessl](https://tessl.io/registry/skills/github/Donchitos/Claude-Code-Game-Studios/ux-design)). Behaviour Interactive approves information hierarchy and navigation flows before high-fidelity work, while changes still "cost minutes" ([Adobe](https://blog.adobe.com/en/publish/2020/03/17/how-behaviour-interactive-is-levelling-up-player-experience-using-ux-best-practices)). Games user research picks a method from the research objective and the stage of development ([Steve Bromley](https://gamesuserresearch.com/five-essential-playtests-throughout-development/)): a study plan states objectives, then method, then the tasks players get ([Games User Research](https://gamesuserresearch.com/designing-a-games-user-research-study/)); RITE fixes issues as soon as they are found and retests with the next participant ([GUR methods](https://gamesuserresearch.com/games-user-research-methods/)); telemetry answers questions such as whether a puzzle is completed in the intended time ([Game Analytics, ch. 14](https://cmps-people.ok.ubc.ca/bowenhui/game/readings/gameAnalyticsCh14.pdf)); and regular small tests tied to design decisions beat one large beta ([Wireframe](https://wireframe.raspberrypi.com/articles/how-to-playtest-your-game-on-a-budget)). Microsoft's Halo 3 research analyzed **more than 3,000 hours of play from about 600 players**, and heatmaps of players wandering led Bungie to change terrain ([Wired](https://wirednews.pages.dev/2007/08/ff-halo-2)). For tiny teams, Brian Cronin's GDC 2026 slides say "3 is enough* testers per round" in most cases and ask for at least one action per playtest (practitioner experience) ([Cronin](https://media.gdcvault.com/gdc2026/Slides/Cronin_Brian_PlaytestingProcessForUltraSmallTeams.pdf)); the golden rule of observation is "do not help", and "vague playtests produce vague feedback" ([GTSTU](https://gtstu.com/playtest-indie-game-solo-developer/)).

Game feel, in Steve Swink's definition, is "real-time control of virtual objects in a simulated space, with interactions emphasised by polish", adjustable through input, response, context, polish, metaphor and rules ([Pichlmair & Johansen](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2011.09201); [Wikiwand](https://www.wikiwand.com/en/articles/Game_feel)). Juice is the layered feedback shown in Jonasson and Purho's "Juice it or lose it" (GDC Europe 2012) and Jan Willem Nijman's "Art of Screenshake", which adds about 30 small tweaks such as knockback, hit pause, recoil and permanence to a plain shooter ([Game Juice](https://gamejuice.pages.dev/resources/juice-it-or-lose-it); [Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/vlambeer-co-founder-shares-advice-on-building-better-action-games)). Squirrel Eiserloh's trauma model scales shake with trauma squared or cubed and drives it with smooth noise ([Bevy example](https://bevy.org/examples/camera/2d-screen-shake/)), and overused shake causes fatigue and motion sickness ([Game Juice](https://gamejuice.pages.dev/articles/screen-shake-guide)). Forgiveness mechanics such as coyote time and jump buffering make correct inputs succeed ([Maddy Thorson](https://maddymakesgames.com/articles/celeste_and_forgiveness/)); Celeste's coyote window is reported as 5 frames by press and wiki sources (the notes flag conflicting values) ([GameRant](https://gamerant.com/celeste-coyote-time-mechanic-platforming-impact-hidden-mechanics/)), and one guide recommends a 100–150 ms jump buffer (single source) ([Game Juice](https://gamejuice.pages.dev/articles/coyote-time-input-buffering)). Latency studies put the **average minimum noticeable latency near 85 ms and the average maximum tolerable near 600 ms**, below 100 ms for every genre, with expert players perceiving about 15 ms; the search summary did not tie each figure to one paper ([WPI 20-02](https://ftp.cs.wpi.edu/pub/techreports/pdf/20-02.pdf); [Claypool et al.](https://web.cs.wpi.edu/~claypool/papers/spaz-22/paper-preprint.pdf)). Research is more cautious than the talks: Hicks found juiciness effects on player experience "nuanced" ([Hicks thesis](https://core.ac.uk/download/163027665.pdf)), which supports the player-experience note's warning that juice amplifies a fun loop but cannot create one.

Onboarding is judged on whether players reach the core fun quickly and learn by doing. Sources advise limiting what players must attend to and sizing the tutorial to the game's complexity ([Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/best-practices-for-a-successful-ftue-first-time-user-experience-)), teaching core mechanics before multiplayer, rewards and ranking ([Overwolf](https://dev.overwolf.com/ow-native/guides/product-guidelines/onboarding/ftue)), making the first 15 minutes engaging and foreshadowing the rest, as Sid Meier put it ([Filament Games](https://www.filamentgames.com/blog/how-design-first-five-minutes-your-game)), and instrumenting every tutorial step as a funnel ([GameAnalytics](https://gameanalytics.com/blog/tips-for-a-great-first-time-user-experience-ftue-in-f2p-games)). Accessibility has three main references. The Game Accessibility Guidelines' basic tier includes remappable controls, no essential information by colour alone, subtitles for important speech and a readable default font ([GAG](https://gameaccessibilityguidelines.com/basic/)). The Xbox Accessibility Guidelines, version 3.2 of June 2023, have 23 numbered guidelines ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/gaming/accessibility/guidelines)), and the contrast guideline cites **4.5:1 for text, 3:1 for large text and 7:1 for a high-contrast mode** ([XAG 102](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/gaming/accessibility/xbox-accessibility-guidelines/102)). AbleGamers' 22 APX design patterns are explicitly not "a checklist" ([AbleGamers](https://accessible.games/apx)). Expert heuristic sets catch many usability issues before playtests, but HEP's 43 heuristics proved too long for evaluators to memorize ([Scitepress](https://scitepress.org/PublishedPapers/2018/66956/pdf/index.html)) and its successor PLAY counts 48 or 50 depending on the source ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/978-3-642-02774-1_60)); neither replaces watching players.

| Deliverable | Contents |
|---|---|
| Target-player profile | 1–3 short personas: genre experience, session context, platform and input device, skill expectations, accessibility needs |
| Information inventory and input map (per topic) | Everything a mechanic requires the player to perceive, with priority (critical, situational, nice-to-know) and channel (world, HUD, audio, haptic); verb → input per platform, hold vs tap, conflicts, remapping |
| Flow map and HUD hierarchy | Boot → title → menus → play → pause → fail/retry → results, with back paths and clicks to first play; HUD inventory, categories, zones and dynamic behaviours |
| Feel target sheet (one per core verb) | Intent adjectives tied to pillars; 1–3 timestamped reference clips; input semantics, buffer and grace windows; response timing (first visible response, wind-up, active, recovery, cancel); motion curves; feedback layers (animation, VFX, audio, camera, hit stop, knockback, haptics, UI); how small, medium and large events scale; tunables; measured acceptance plus a side-by-side verdict against the references |
| FTUE plan | Ordered list of what must be learned to reach the core loop; for each, where and how it is taught, how mastery is confirmed by doing, what failure looks like; time targets to first control, first core action and first payoff; what is deliberately deferred; a telemetry event per step; skip and replay |
| Playtest plan and report | Objectives tied to pillars or decisions, questions, method, participant profile, script including what the moderator must not say, observation sheet, survey; report with a verdict per objective, issues with evidence, cause, impact, severity and recommendation, positives to keep, and metrics against the last benchmark |
| Accessibility minimum | Remapping, separate volume sliders, subtitles on or offered at first launch, text size, colour-safe palette with redundant cues, shake and flash toggles, difficulty or assist options, hold-to-toggle alternatives |

These contents are the player-experience note's synthesis from the sources above; its timing targets are design targets, not standards.

### Production, technology and QA: gates with written exit criteria

Most studios run concept, a greenlight, pre-production ending in a vertical slice, production to alpha (feature complete) and beta (content complete, stabilizing), then a release candidate, but definitions vary enough that sources tell teams to write each gate's exit criteria down ([gamedevproducer.com](https://gamedevproducer.com/posts/what-is-a-game-milestone-alpha-beta-gold/); [Game-Ace](https://game-ace.com/blog/game-development-stages/)). The gate that bears on taste is the end of pre-production. Mark Cerny's Method, applied at Naughty Dog and Insomniac, asks for a "publishable first playable" at "publishable quality" with defined scope, "a touch of variety" and art direction in place, tested two to five times with observation, and treats a project that cannot reach it as a cancellation candidate because production will not fix it ([Cerny Method slides](https://www.slideshare.net/holtt/cerny-method); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_Cerny)). Riot's prototype stage asks whether the game should be made at all, wants "proof that the audience likes the experience via playtesting", and accepts that "a good playtest demonstrates the game direction won't work, and the team either pivots or cancels the project" ([Riot](https://www.riotgames.com/en/r-and-d-office/prototype-building-a-games-substance)), while its goal for prototype art is "ugly but fun" ([Riot](https://www.riotgames.com/en/r-and-d-office/collaborative-creative-in-r-d)). A Cornell course warns that criteria like "the game is fun" are hard to measure and must be made concrete ([Cornell CS 3152](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/courses/cs3152/2024sp/assignments/a5/)). The producer's tools are a MoSCoW list whose Must test is "would the game fail to deliver its one-sentence pitch without this feature?", revisited every couple of weeks and checked against remaining hours ([GTSTU](https://gtstu.com/scope-your-first-indie-game-so-you-actually-ship-it/)), plus milestone documents with concrete acceptance criteria. The technical director owns per-platform budgets for frame rate, memory, load times and streaming, checked "at every stage of production" and agreed with art and design leads ([That's No Moon posting](https://outscal.com/job/studio-technical-director-at-that-s-no-moon-in-united-states)), and keeps prototype code throwaway ([Riot](https://www.riotgames.com/en/r-and-d-office/engineering-in-riot-r-d)). The QA lead runs a four-level severity scheme (crash, blocker, major, minor) kept separate from priority ([Bugnet](https://bugnet.io/blog/bug-severity-classification-for-game-developers); [Packt](https://subscription.packtpub.com/book/game-development/9781803244402/10/ch10lvl1sec49/severity-versus-priority)), beta exit thresholds on crash rate and severity-1 counts, and a certification-style checklist; on Xbox, **title stability causes 38% of console certification failures**, ahead of title integrity at 14% ([Xbox devdocs](https://devdocs.xbox.com/publishing/certification/xr-top-failing.md)).

## Studios that keep quality judge the build, and thirty documents ranked last

The cross-studio evidence on review is consistent: critics look at a playable build or a screening, not a document. Pixar's Braintrust "has no authority", its notes are "not prescriptive", a good note "says what is wrong, what is missing, what isn't clear, what makes no sense", and "it is up to the director alone to determine how to move forward" ([Fast Company](https://www.fastcompany.com/3027135/inside-the-pixar-braintrust)); its dailies normalize showing unfinished work ([Shortform](https://www.shortform.com/blog/pixar-dailies/)), with one secondary account putting the right moment at 25–75% complete (unverified) ([Center Centre](https://medium.com/creating-a-ux-strategy-playbook/goods-bads-and-dailies-lessons-for-conducting-great-critiques-f2089046b274)). Valve's Half-Life cabals were groups of four or five, about half level designers and half programmers ([Valve Developer Wiki](https://developer.valvesoftware.com/wiki/Cabal_process)); late in development Valve realized the game "well, sucked" and rebuilt around playtesting ([GMTK](https://gmtk.substack.com/p/valves-secret-weapon)); for Half-Life 2 an "Overwatch Cabal" judged global quality at alpha ([GDC Vault](https://gdcvault.com/play/1013450/Valve-s-Design-Process-for)); and Chet Faliszek's rule is "Playtesters aren't idiots, it's you" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/valve-s-faliszek-playtesters-aren-t-idiots-it-s-you)). Blizzard's strike teams are "members of the company that aren't directly working on a project" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/paris-gdc-the-rob-pardo-experience)), and Rob Pardo ran the feedback cycle "every couple of weeks" ([GamesBeat](https://gamesbeat.com/game-boss-interview-rob-pardo-says-playtesting-is-critical-to-game-design/3)). Insomniac staff play builds almost every week while each project's creative directors decide what to do with the feedback, and Ted Price stopped second-guessing them because it undercut their authority ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/production/ted-price-explains-the-insomniac-approach-to-creative-leadership)). Riot's champions team playtests at least once a sprint, "followed immediately by a team feedback session" that sets priorities ([Riot](https://www.riotgames.com/en/work-with-us/disciplines/dev-management/dont-go-chasing-waterfall-using-agile-methods-in-creative-development)). Bungie's designers watched playtests ([Bungie GDC 2002](https://www.halo.bungie.org/misc/gdc.2002.haloai/talk.html?page=19)), yet Bungie overrode Microsoft's researchers on Halo 2 matchmaking, which one researcher later called "exactly the right call" ([Polygon via NeoGAF](https://m.neogaf.com/threads/polygon-the-time-i-tried-to-ruin-halo-2.1492093/)). Supergiant states the principle most bluntly: "the game is the design document" ([GamesRadar+](https://www.gamesradar.com/games/hades/the-game-is-the-design-document-hades-2-devs-dont-have-long-elaborate-plans-that-lay-out-the-future-of-the-things-were-making-because-supergiant-is-a-heavily-iterative-studio)), and designing on paper "can barely mean anything" ([GameDaily](https://www.gamedaily.biz/?p=588)). Clinton Keith's agile method puts "Finding the Fun First" ([O'Reilly](https://www.oreilly.com/library/view/agile-game-development/9780136204831/pref02.xhtml)), with a sprint goal to show "a game that's more fun than the one shown from the previous sprint" that management "OKs… or axes" ([Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/business/fun-over-features)).

Read together, these cases give six rules: review the build, include reviewers from outside the feature team, diagnose instead of prescribing, keep notes advisory with one named owner deciding, settle disputes with observed play, and critique early and often. The same cases document how review goes wrong. Ubisoft's central editorial team grew from about 10–15 people to about 80 over eight years by Serge Hascoët's account ([Game Informer](https://www.gameinformer.com/2018/10/01/ubisofts-creative-head-talks-the-future-of-assassins-creed-and-splinter-cell)), about 100 by VGC's ([VGC](https://www.videogameschronicle.com/news/ubisoft-revamps-editorial-team/)), and after Ghost Recon Breakpoint's weak reception was restructured in 2020 "in pursuit of more unique games", with franchise VPs given authority to decide independently ([GamesRadar+](https://gamesradar.com/ubisoft-shakes-up-executive-editorial-team-in-pursuit-of-more-unique-games)). A central reviewer applying one formula homogenizes. Miyamoto's habit of "upending the tea table" late in development is one he said in 2020 he had broken, now reworking only when he can see how the whole game will turn out ([Nintendo Life](https://nintendolife.com/news/2020/03/miyamoto_has_broken_his_habit_of_completely_reworking_games_mid-development)), which places wholesale rework at early gates. Consensus groups bottleneck as scope grows, as Valve found, and design documents drift from the game, as Deus Ex's did.

The most direct evidence on role documents comes from Claude-Code-Game-Studios (CCGS), an open-source Claude Code skill set and the closest prior art to GameDirector. It defines **49 role agents in three tiers and 28 named director gates** such as CD-PILLARS, AD-ART-BIBLE and CD-PLAYTEST ([README](https://github.com/Donchitos/Claude-Code-Game-Studios/blob/main/README.md); [director-gates.md](https://github.com/Donchitos/Claude-Code-Game-Studios/blob/main/.claude/docs/director-gates.md)). Its creative director, art director, game designer, audio director and UX designer have read, write, edit and web-search tools but Bash disallowed, so **none of the creative roles can launch the game** ([creative-director.md](https://github.com/Donchitos/Claude-Code-Game-Studios/blob/main/.claude/agents/creative-director.md); [art-director.md](https://github.com/Donchitos/Claude-Code-Game-Studios/blob/main/.claude/agents/art-director.md)), and CD-PLAYTEST reviews a written playtest report against pillars ([cd-playtest.md](https://github.com/Donchitos/Claude-Code-Game-Studios/blob/main/.claude/docs/director-gates/cd-playtest.md)). When the maintainer built games both ways, the `minimal` path wrote one document, the brief, before the first line of code, while the `standard` path wrote 30 documents in 58 minutes: "All of them produced a running, tested game. Two blind reviewers and a human playtest then ranked the `standard` build **last** — the extra 29 documents bought traceability, not a better game" ([README](https://github.com/Donchitos/Claude-Code-Game-Studios/blob/main/README.md)). The changelog adds that the heavier tier "cost several times more to reach working code, did not produce a better result, and gave nothing back when someone else picked the project up", which is why the no-gates mode became the default in 1.2.0 ([CHANGELOG](https://github.com/Donchitos/Claude-Code-Game-Studios/blob/main/CHANGELOG.md)). The caveats are real: the result is maintainer-reported, the sample is tiny (one rigor comparison, three ideas in the brief test) and the blind-review protocol is unpublished. What did improve results in the same project points the same way. A brief built from questions about the look, the first minute, the challenge and what to keep or avoid beat a hand-written brief in all three blind comparisons, and the project's look-checking rule says a story that changes anything player-visible "is not closed until the game has been launched, observed, and a screenshot retained", because "a parse check is not a run"; every handover ends "Not yet played by a person" ([README](https://github.com/Donchitos/Claude-Code-Game-Studios/blob/main/README.md); [run-and-observe.md](https://github.com/Donchitos/Claude-Code-Game-Studios/blob/main/.claude/docs/run-and-observe.md)).

Anthropic's harness experiment for long-running application builds shows the same gap on a game. A solo 20-minute, $9 run of a retro 2D game maker produced entities that did not respond to input, with the "wiring… broken with no visible indication"; a six-hour, $200 planner–generator–evaluator harness produced a working play mode, yet the character overlapped platforms, a wall could not be jumped, and the evaluator missed layout and play-feel problems ([Anthropic Engineering](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/harness-design-long-running-apps)). Large-sample evidence explains why taste gates matter. A coding of about 200 Gamasutra postmortems found that most root causes are "related to people, not technologies" ([Politowski et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2009.02440v2)); a follow-up on 200 projects and 927 problems put 45% in management practice ([Ullmann, Politowski et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.06183)); and the Game Outcomes Project found that teams with a strong, shared vision they care about score better with critics, though only weakly better on timeliness ([Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/business/the-game-outcomes-project-part-3-game-development-factors)).

The asker's result fits this pattern exactly. GameDirector's build and verdict steps enforce what alpha and beta gates enforce (features exist, tests pass, the build runs), and those gates say nothing about whether the game is good. The defensible reading is not "documents are bad". Falsifiable rules such as an art bible's colour roles, an audio priority model or a feel sheet's response target are exactly what make a build review sharp, and a brief built from the right questions measurably helped CCGS. The lesson is that document volume does not buy quality, and that a role which reviews only text against pillars cannot see the failures that made the asker's game look bad and play flat. The usual argument for heavier documentation is traceability and handover; CCGS measured both and found the handover benefit was nil.

## AI reviewers reliably catch broken things and rarely catch good ones

An autonomous pipeline can only own taste if its reviewers can see what a person sees, and the evaluation literature draws a sharp line. Automated agents are good at finding broken things: an LLM testing agent completed 95% of tasks on two commercial MMORPGs ([TITAN](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.22170)), frontier vision-language models wrote useful bug reports for up to about half of real-world glitches ([VideoGameQA-Bench](https://papers.nips.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/hash/db2cf8f479a96cb83329f5e193318a29-Abstract-Datasets_and_Benchmarks_Track.html)), and Anthropic's evaluator caught concrete functional bugs by clicking through the live app ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/harness-design-long-running-apps)). They are weak at fine visual detail: on trivial geometric tasks such as counting overlapping circles, four frontier VLMs averaged **58.57%** and the best reached **74.01%**, against near-perfect human accuracy ([VLMs are blind, ACCV 2024](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/ACCV2024/papers/Rahmanzadehgervi_Vision_language_models_are_blind_ACCV_2024_paper.pdf)), and VideoGameQA-Bench found models struggle with subtle regressions and with localizing glitches in longer clips. Most telling for this asker, **V-GameGym's 2,219 Pygame tasks found that top models succeed only about 45% of the time and that "code generation accuracy does not guarantee game quality"**, because models scored well on logic while producing poor animation or unresponsive UI ([V-GameGym, ACL Findings 2026](https://aclanthology.org/2026.findings-acl.276/)). That is the "worked on paper, looked bad" failure, measured.

Fun is the weakest judgment of all. Collins et al. compared language models with about 450 human judgments of more than 100 novel board games and found model agreement on funness lower and more "jagged" across model families than agreement on payoff and fairness, with step-by-step reasoning helping; the human ratings came from novices before they played ([Collins et al., NeurIPS 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.10930)). The notes found no study that validates an AI fun score against player data for real-time games. Difficulty is the exception: Roohi et al. combined deep-RL play with a simulated player population to predict per-level pass rate and churn, using data from 168 levels and 95,266 players that linked the two ([Roohi et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.12061)). Feel is best measured through proxies such as input latency and the presence of feedback, not through model opinion. Audio cannot be judged by listening at all, because "Claude can't actually hear" ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/harness-design-long-running-apps)). Richer inputs do not help automatically either: in AVR-Agent, custom assets and audio-visual feedback had no significant effect on generated games, and **best-of-k initial drafts generally beat k extra refinement iterations**, on a small set of five games and five animations judged by an evaluator the same author designed ([AVR-Agent](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.00632)). Working developers share the skepticism and rely "almost exclusively" on manual playtesting ([Politowski et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.06431)).

The same literature says what makes an AI reviewer useful rather than a rubber stamp. Agents "reliably skew positive when grading their own work", and out of the box Claude found real issues and then approved the work anyway, whereas tuning a separate evaluator to be skeptical "turns out to be far more tractable" ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/harness-design-long-running-apps)). Anthropic's evaluator weighted design quality and originality above craft and functionality, explicitly penalizing template defaults and "AI slop" such as "purple gradients over white cards", and was calibrated with few-shot scored examples and by reading its logs wherever it diverged from the human; prompt wording had side effects, with "museum quality" causing visual convergence. LLM judges show position, verbosity and self-preference biases, mitigated by swapping order and judging with a different model family ([MT-Bench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05685)), and judges of role-play fidelity reach about 69% against humans' 90.8% ([PersonaEval](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.10014)). Multi-agent debate does not reliably beat self-consistency or ensembling ([Smit et al., ICML 2024](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v235/smit24a.html)), identical same-model debaters undermine it while model heterogeneity helps ([Zhang et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.08788)), and multi-agent failures cluster in specification and verification ([MAST](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.13657)). Divergent ideation is the kinder case: role-play discussion raised originality on divergent-thinking benchmarks, a weak proxy for game design ([LLM Discussion](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.06373)). CCGS contributes mechanics worth copying: gate definitions read only by the spawned reviewer in its own context, fixed verdict words with NOT ASSESSED ranked below CONCERNS because "a gate that could not look has approved nothing", the strictest verdict winning, and the rule that helper scripts "emit observations, never verdicts" ([director-gates.md](https://github.com/Donchitos/Claude-Code-Game-Studios/blob/main/.claude/docs/director-gates.md); [CCGS CLAUDE.md](https://github.com/Donchitos/Claude-Code-Game-Studios/blob/main/CLAUDE.md)).

| Tier | What it can decide | Role checks that fall here |
|---|---|---|
| Measured by code (may block) | Facts against thresholds | Build launches and responds; crash and soak; frame time and memory against budget; input-to-first-response frames and buffer windows under scripted input; which feedback channels fire per action; palette extracted from captures against the art bible's hex table; grayscale histogram for muddy midtones; figure/ground contrast; text size, overlap and off-screen elements; contrast ratios; colour-vision-deficiency simulation; pairwise silhouette overlap; every gameplay event mapped to an audio event; integrated LUFS and true peak; variation counts and back-to-back repeats; voice budget; subtitle and glossary coverage; string length; launch-to-control time; seeded bot completability, failure heatmaps and difficulty curves |
| Judged by AI on captures (provisional notes) | Comparisons against a reference, with a checklist | Side-by-side cohesion with the visual target; placeholder or default-engine look; read order and focal hierarchy; "stock asset pack" or genre-default look; UI text against the tone document; script review for exposition and voice; ludonarrative mismatches in play traces; fun hypotheses reasoned from bot traces (decisions, tension, pacing) |
| Needs a person | Experience | Whether it is fun and worth replaying; feel perceived on real hardware; beauty and final taste; listening to sound and music; comprehension and mental models; emotional arc; motion sickness; accessibility with disabled players |

The tiers are the notes' synthesis from the evidence above. The art and audio notes found no validated numeric thresholds for screenshot readability or per-category asset loudness, so every measured check must be calibrated against the game's own target frames and bible rather than a universal number. Feel needs motion, since single screenshots cannot show hit stop, shake or particles, so the minimum input for a feel review is a short clip or frame strip around an input plus an event log of which feedback channels fired.

## Wiring the roles into GameDirector's phases

The evidence turns into four contributions per role, fitted to GameDirector's one-step-per-session design. In ideation and brainstorming each role supplies the questions the session asks the person and challenges answers with. Each role keeps at most one short living wiki page of rules an artifact can fail, only as deep as play needs. Each role has a review lens with defined inputs (captures from fixed states, clips, event logs, bot runs) that measures first and judges second, in a context separate from the builder. And a single director lens records a disposition for every note: fix now, schedule, cut, or won't fix with a reason. The mapping below is this report's synthesis of the notes, not documented studio practice. It stays genre-, engine-, platform- and tracker-agnostic by expressing checks as measurements on captures and logs, choosing platform numbers (loudness, contrast, budgets) during plan from the target platform, switching lenses on by need (a combat roster only for games with opposition, an economy sheet only for priced choices, the narrative minimum kit for story-light games), and writing findings as tickets in whatever tracker the game uses.

| Role lens | Ideation | Brainstorm and consolidate | Plan and build | Verdict and polish review |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Director (creative/game) | Vision, fantasy, pillars and anti-pillars, kill condition, 3–5 directions, slice bars | Challenges every answer for pillar fit; one canonical pillar wording; identity invariants | Pillar-proximity cut pass on features; rate-limits its own change requests | Disposes of every note; runs Schell's gap check on playtest evidence; overturns direction only at ideation or the slice |
| Design (systems, level, combat, economy) | Core loop, verbs and resources, the slice's space and pacing beat | One-page system specs, beat charts, enemy roster, source/sink sheet | Tunables in one file; seeded bot hooks; metrics map | Bot runs and clips: completability, failure heatmaps, time per beat against the chart, tells and roles, cost-curve and archetype sims |
| Feel | 1–2 adjectives and 1–3 reference clips per core verb | Feel target sheet per verb | Response frames, buffer windows and feedback layers as acceptance; layers toggled off and on | Input-to-response logs and channel coverage (measured); clips beside references (provisional); felt on hardware by the person |
| Art (art director, concept, UI art, animation, VFX, tech art) | Slogan, 3–5 rule-pillars, purpose-sorted reference board, 2–3 directions for the person | Art bible grows per topic: hex colour-role table, layer bands, silhouettes, UI style guide, animation and VFX rules, invariants | Visual target built first; budgets; style enforced by shaders; normalization of sourced or generated assets; capture states | Palette, value histogram, contrast, silhouette and colour-blindness checks (measured); side-by-side cohesion with the target and placeholder scan (provisional); beauty (person) |
| Audio | What the player must do by ear; 1–2 pillars; references and anti-references; voice scope | Handbook: palette per category, signature sounds, priority tiers, music scheme, UI sound family | Event list; house processing chain; loudness target; variation minimums | Event coverage, LUFS and true peak, repeats, voice budget (measured); listening (person) |
| Narrative and world | Fantasy line, tone words and anti-words, setting sentence, twist | Tone document, lean bible, character cards, naming rules, glossary; need test on every entry | String tables with IDs and length limits; barks per trigger | Glossary, length and continuity checks (measured); tone and ludonarrative notes (provisional); can players restate premise and tone (person) |
| UX, onboarding and accessibility | Target player, session context, input device, access needs | Information inventory and channel per topic; input map; FTUE outline | Flow map; greybox UI landing with each mechanic; settings spec | Launch-to-control time, text size, overlap, contrast, settings presence (measured); heuristic rubric (provisional); comprehension (person) |
| User research | What the slice must prove, per pillar | Research questions per pillar | Telemetry events per feature | Designs the slice playtest; analyzes the person's notes and recordings |
| Producer | Pitch sentence | Scope flag per topic | MoSCoW against the pitch; Must list against remaining sessions | Cut list; disposition log |
| Technical director | Feasibility of the chosen style on the target engine and platform | Budget implications per topic | Capture, logging and bot tooling (from setup); budgets; profiling | Frame time, memory and load against budget |
| QA | Test strategy | Edge cases per topic | Smoke and soak harness; severity and priority scheme | Regression; stability soak; compliance-style final checklist |

**Ideation gives every aspect a north star the person chooses.** GameDirector's ideation already produces a vision, 3–4 pillars, anti-pillars, a kill condition, 3–5 compared directions and a slice definition with a quality bar per aspect. The role evidence argues for adding a north star per aspect at the same sitting, and for making the person's choice between two or three named directions per aspect, with references they supply or approve, the anchor for every later review; CCGS's AD-CONCEPT-VISUAL gate has the right shape, asking for directions that each carry a one-line rule such as "everything must move" ([ad-concept-visual.md](https://github.com/Donchitos/Claude-Code-Game-Studios/blob/main/.claude/docs/director-gates/ad-concept-visual.md)). The technical director's lens should steer the art direction toward a style the pipeline can hit consistently; the art note's inference for an AI pipeline is a small role-assigned palette, a fixed pixel or texel density and flat or low-poly shading enforced in code, because coherence beats fidelity for small teams. Divergence at this stage is where best-of-k generation and multi-persona discussion have support, provided the losing directions' reasons are recorded. For the slice's quality bars, the evidence argues against leaving art and audio at placeholder: Riot's "ugly but fun" governs prototypes, but Cerny's gate asks for art direction in place at publishable quality, and one definition of a slice is near-final quality in every discipline, although another source describes slices as rough and unpolished. A defensible setting is feel at production, one visual-target area at production with the rest representative, and audio representative with signature sounds near final.

**Brainstorm lets the lenses supply the grilling questions while the wiki holds rules.** Brainstorm runs one topic per session and never prototypes, so its output is rules framed as hypotheses that the first build must prove. Each topic is grilled with the questions of every lens it touches. A character or enemy topic asks what its silhouette and role are, which colour role and value band it occupies, what it sounds like and at which priority tier, what tell precedes its attack and for how long, what personality its barks carry, and what the player must perceive to deal with it and through which channel. A world topic asks for the genre default and this game's departure, what each invented rule costs and who feels it in play, and whether each entry changes something the player sees, hears, reads or does. Answers are challenged against the vision and against the discipline's existing rules, and every rule cites the pillar it serves and states how a capture could fail it. Volume is the enemy here: the CCGS measurement and the Deus Ex drift both warn against letting the wiki become the product.

**Consolidate writes one taste contract, split by who can judge it.** Beyond completeness, consolidate should check cross-discipline consistency: the art bible's colour roles match UI, VFX and faction palettes, no information is colour-only, every player-critical sound has a visual cue, the tone document has touchstones for writing, art, music and UI, and pillar wording is identical everywhere. The fun checklist should then be split into the three tiers so each item states whether it is measured, judged provisionally by AI, or waiting on a person, with examples such as a feedback-coverage rule (every player action and hit fires at least two channels), a first-control time target, a difficulty-curve shape from bot runs, and person-only items such as "wants to play again". Richness targets become per-discipline counts the build can be checked against: distinct enemy roles and silhouettes, signature sounds, bark variants scaled to trigger frequency, and Cerny's "touch of variety" in the slice. Before any build exists, the person can also label a small calibration set (their references as "our game", engine defaults and stock assets as "not our game") to give the AI art reviewer the few-shot anchors Anthropic credits with reducing drift. That step is an inference; no study has measured how many examples a game needs.

**Plan sets budgets, enforces style in code and schedules the visual target first.** The technical director fixes per-platform budgets and a capture protocol that records the same states every build: the beauty-corner view, a typical play moment, peak effects load, a menu, and the HUD over the brightest and darkest areas. Style enforcement belongs in code wherever possible, through a palette-quantize or outline shader, a fixed lighting rig and, for audio, a house processing chain with per-category loudness normalization, because mixed-origin assets (generated, library or hand-made) are what produce the asset-flip look. The producer runs MoSCoW against the pitch sentence and checks the Must list against remaining sessions. The walking skeleton should put the visual target and the core verb's feel target among its first features, as Overwatch modeled Torbjörn first and Behaviour locks hierarchy before high fidelity, so the art bible and feel sheet are proved or revised before content multiplies. Each ticket carries its measured acceptance (response frames, feedback channels, palette, band, event coverage, loudness) and lists its provisional and person-only criteria as NOT ASSESSED until someone checks them.

**Build keeps the builder honest by handing judgment to a separate lens.** Each feature session implements test-first, launches the game into the affected state, captures, and retains evidence tied to the code it proves; a player-visible change is not done until that has happened. The builder does not grade its own work. A fresh-context lens reads the captures and logs, measured failures block, and provisional notes go to the tracker in what/why/fix form for the director lens to dispose of. Feel features toggle each feedback layer off and on to show it earns its place and log their tuned values. Every few features, a short "dailies" review of the capture reel against the visual target and reference clips catches drift before it compounds, the agent analogue of Riot's per-sprint playtest and Insomniac's weekly build play.

**Verdict runs Braintrust notes on the build, and the slice belongs to the person.** The core-loop verdict reads seeded bot runs and clips through the design, feel and UX lenses (completability, difficulty curve, decisions per stretch of play, input response, feedback coverage), uses Koster's, Schell's and Meier's questions as reasoning prompts on traces, and marks fun provisional. The slice verdict is the Cerny gate: is the slice publishable in every discipline, with defined scope, a touch of variety and art direction in place? Every discipline lens writes problem-only notes from captures (what is wrong, missing, unclear, makes no sense), the director lens disposes of each, and the user-research lens plans the human playtest as a short cold session with two to five objectives tied to pillars, assumptions written beforehand, think-aloud, the rule "do not help", an observation sheet and a five-question survey. That playtest is the only reliable fun and feel signal in the pipeline, and pivot or kill is a legitimate outcome, which is what GameDirector's kill condition exists for. The full verdict is the Overwatch Cabal: fresh-context reviewers who did not watch the work grow judge the whole game's cohesion and pacing against the identity invariants. The final verdict is QA's certification-style pass, stability soak first given its share of certification failures, then save integrity, settings persistence, accessibility settings and loudness compliance.

**Polish runs one pass per role, guarded by identity invariants.** Feel is tuned one parameter at a time against reference clips. Art gets a cohesion pass (normalize outliers, unify outline and density), lighting and colour grade per area, and noise reduction in the play layer. Audio gets the worst-case-scene mix, a variation and repetition pass, a missing-sound sweep and loudness compliance. Narrative gets tone, continuity and localization-readiness passes; UX gets the accessibility settings minimum and FTUE timing against the funnel; the technical director checks performance against budget. Identity invariants keep polish from sanding off what makes the game recognizable. The person's cheapest high-value input here is blind A/B choice between before-and-after captures or builds, which is less biased than open critique.

Two documented review failures apply directly to agents. A central reviewer applying a generic formula homogenizes games, as Ubisoft concluded, so every critic lens judges against this game's pillars, references and invariants and weights distinctiveness explicitly, as Anthropic's rubric weighted originality. Late wholesale rework is costly, as Miyamoto concluded, so the director lens's power to overturn direction belongs at ideation and the slice verdict, with later phases limited to fixes. A panel of same-model critics debating adds cost without evidence of benefit; the supported shape is a few lenses with deliberately different criteria and inputs, each in a fresh context, and one decider.

## Conclusion

Taste was missing from the asker's run not because no document named it, but because no reviewer with eyes on the running game, and no person calibrating that reviewer, ever had a say. The research supports a sharper claim than "add the roles": adding role documents and sign-offs that read text would most likely reproduce CCGS's result, more cost and no better game. What the traditional roles actually offer an AI pipeline is what an experienced person carries in their head: falsifiable rules a capture can fail, measurements that turn readability, palette, response and loudness into facts, and review questions a skeptical, separate evaluator can apply to frames, clips and bot traces. Seen that way, the art director's mood board, colour palette and art bible matter less as documents than as the reference frames and hex table a reviewer compares every capture against.

The second implication concerns autonomy itself. Every system in the notes that reports good creative results either keeps a person at decision points or says plainly that the game has not been played by one, and no study validates an AI judgment of fun or beauty for real-time games. A fully autonomous run can be made coherent, readable and responsive, because those properties are measurable, but "fun" and "good-looking" should be reported as hypotheses until a person has played. The highest-leverage choices are therefore early and cheap: a style the pipeline can hit consistently and enforce in code, a visual target and feel target built first, and four short moments of a person's time (choose, label, play, compare). How few of those moments suffice is still unknown, since no study has measured checkpoint placement or calibration size for AI-built games.
